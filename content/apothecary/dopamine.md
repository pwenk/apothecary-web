In 1989 a rat in Michigan had lost most of the dopamine in its reward system. Left alone, it would starve beside a full bowl. Drip sugar on its tongue, though, and it licked its lips with obvious pleasure.

That rat explains why you can scroll for an hour and enjoy none of it. By the end of this page you'll know what dopamine actually does, why uncertainty makes it sing, and why the fashionable cure aimed at it misses the target.

Start with the recipe, because your body cooks dopamine from breakfast.

## From tyrosine to want

Dopamine begins as tyrosine, an amino acid from the protein you eat (your liver can also make it from phenylalanine). An enzyme called tyrosine hydroxylase turns tyrosine into L-DOPA. This is the slow step, the bottleneck, and it needs iron and a helper molecule called BH4. Because it's a bottleneck, eating more tyrosine doesn't simply mean more dopamine.

A second enzyme, which runs on vitamin B6, snips L-DOPA into dopamine. One more step, using vitamin C and copper, turns some of that dopamine into noradrenaline, the alertness signal.

Dopamine can't cross from blood into brain. L-DOPA can. In the late 1950s the Swedish pharmacologist Arvid Carlsson drained rabbits of their brain chemicals with the drug reserpine, watched them go limp, then got them moving again with L-DOPA. That pointed straight at Parkinson's disease and won him a share of the 2000 Nobel Prize. Oliver Sacks later wrote *Awakenings* about patients frozen for decades who came alive on the same drug.

So dopamine moves you, literally. Where it goes after it's made decides what it moves.

## Four roads out of the midbrain

Most dopamine cells sit in a small patch of the midbrain and in the hypothalamus. Four main roads leave from there:

- **Nigrostriatal:** substantia nigra to the striatum. Movement. Lose these cells and you get Parkinson's.
- **Mesolimbic:** the ventral tegmental area (VTA) to the nucleus accumbens. Motivation, craving, the pull toward things.
- **Mesocortical:** the VTA to the prefrontal cortex. Focus, holding a plan in mind.
- **Tuberoinfundibular:** hypothalamus to pituitary, a few millimetres long. Here dopamine is a brake on prolactin, the hormone that floods you after orgasm (more on that in the [next essay](/apothecary/prolactin)).

The mesolimbic road is the one with the neon signs. For decades everyone thought it carried pleasure.

## Wanting without liking

In 1954 James Olds and Peter Milner wired rats so a lever press zapped this area. The rats pressed and pressed. "Pleasure centre," everyone said.

Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson spent the following decades taking that idea apart. Their dopamine-depleted rats still made the happy face rats make for sugar (rhythmic tongue pushes, lip licking). They simply wouldn't go and get it. In 2003 Berridge's lab tested the reverse: mice bred with about 70 percent more dopamine between their nerve cells. They ran to sugar faster, learned quicker, ignored distractions. Their faces showed no extra delight.

**Dopamine is the wanting, and liking is a separate system**, built from small "hedonic hotspots" that run on the brain's own opioids and cannabinoids. Berridge called dopamine's job incentive salience: it paints a thing as worth reaching for.

> Dopamine is the wanting you feel in your hand before the thing is in it.

That gap between the two systems is where most modern misery lives. A man can want his phone intensely and like it barely at all. And the wanting gets sharper when the payoff is unsure.

## The error that teaches

In Fribourg, Switzerland, Wolfram Schultz recorded single dopamine cells in monkeys getting drops of juice. An unexpected drop: the cells burst. Then a light came on before each drop, again and again. Soon the cells fired at the light and ignored the juice, which had become old news. If the light came on and the juice never arrived, the cells went quiet below their normal hum at the exact moment the juice was due.

In 1997 Schultz, Peter Dayan and Read Montague put a name on it: reward prediction error, what you got minus what you expected. It matches the maths that machine-learning researchers use to teach programs by trial and error. Dopamine pays out for surprise. Better than expected: burst. As expected: nothing. Worse: a dip that feels like disappointment.

That is why the first bite is the best one, and why every pleasure you can predict goes flat.

## Why novelty, porn and slot machines hit it

Schultz's lab found a second signal in 2003. When a cue promised juice only some of the time, a slow ramp of firing built up while the monkey waited, and it was strongest at 50:50, maximum uncertainty. A slot machine is a 50:50 engine with lights. In a 2009 Cambridge study, near misses on a slot machine, which pay nothing, recruited the same brain areas as wins and made people keener to play on.

Novelty works on the same circuit. In 2006 Nico Bunzeck and Emrah Düzel showed people pictures in a scanner, and their midbrain dopamine region lit up for images they'd never seen before.

Sex shows it most plainly. In 1997 Dennis Fiorino and Anthony Phillips measured dopamine in male rats' nucleus accumbens. It rose with a receptive female and fell back to baseline once the male was spent. Then a new female arrived. Dopamine climbed again, and so did the rat. Biologists call it the Coolidge effect, after an old joke about the president and his wife touring a chicken farm.

Internet porn is the Coolidge effect on infinite scroll: a new partner per click, every scene chosen to beat the last. In 2014 Valerie Voon's Cambridge group scanned men with compulsive sexual behaviour. Explicit clips lit up their craving network more than in other men, and they reported more desire, yet they didn't like the clips any more. Wanting without liking, in a scanner. (The study measured brain activity, not dopamine itself.)

Jamal Dinkoui, the Berbarian Wizard, builds part of his testosterone advice on this. In his view porn trains arousal to a stream of cues that one real partner can't match, and he tells men in his restoration plan to drop it entirely. He notes that a large survey of young men linked problem porn use, though not masturbation frequency, with erection trouble. That is an association in a survey, so it doesn't prove which causes which. His conditioning argument fits the dopamine science well. The hard proof in humans isn't in yet.

Some brains find the ordinary world harder to want.

## ADHD, a quieter reward road

In 2009 Nora Volkow's team at Brookhaven scanned 53 unmedicated adults with ADHD and 44 without. The ADHD group had fewer dopamine transporters and fewer D2/D3 receptors along the mesolimbic road, and the shortfall tracked how inattentive they were.

One reading: tasks that aren't intrinsically exciting don't pay out enough to hold attention, while novelty and urgency still do. Stimulant medicines raise dopamine at the synapse: methylphenidate blocks its recycling, amphetamines push more of it out. ADHD is far more than one molecule, and it runs strongly in families. There's more in [the ADHD signal](/signals/adhd).

Which brings us to the people trying to turn dopamine down on purpose.

## The dopamine fast that starves nothing

In 2019 the California psychiatrist Cameron Sepah popularised the "dopamine fast": stretches of time away from phones, porn, sugar and other easy rewards. He later told the *New York Times* the name was a catchy title. His method came from cognitive behavioural therapy and its old tool, stimulus control: fewer triggers around you means fewer learned cravings firing.

Then Silicon Valley took the name literally. People skipped meals, music and conversation, hoping to "reset" their receptors. Harvard's Peter Grinspoon pointed out in 2020 that you have no store of dopamine to drain and refill. Your brain keeps making it whatever you do, and a dull afternoon doesn't make it drop.

Heavy stimulant drug use does change dopamine receptors, and that is well documented. A weekend without Instagram hasn't been shown to do anything comparable. **The useful part of the fast is behavioural**: break the cue, break the habit loop. The molecule was never the thing being starved.

## What holds up and what doesn't

- **Holds up (strong):** the synthesis chain, the pathways, wanting versus liking in animals, reward prediction error in animals and humans.
- **Holds up (good, mostly animal):** uncertainty and novelty amplify dopamine signals. The Coolidge effect in rats.
- **Plausible, not proven:** that porn rewires human arousal through dopamine. That ADHD is mainly a reward-pathway problem.
- **Doesn't hold up:** dopamine as the "pleasure molecule". Dopamine fasting as a chemical reset.

## Before you try anything

L-DOPA, mucuna (a bean that contains it) and the Parkinson's drugs that mimic dopamine are real pharmacology. Dopamine-mimicking drugs carry label warnings about compulsive gambling, shopping and sex. Stimulants are prescription medicines for a reason, and anything dopaminergic can clash with MAO-inhibitor antidepressants. Talk to a doctor before you change a molecule this central.

Remember the shortest of the four roads, the few millimetres from hypothalamus to pituitary? There dopamine does a quieter job: it sits on a hormone that makes milk, rises after every orgasm, and in excess shuts desire down. Next: prolactin.
