Ask people on these drugs what changed first, and many talk about a silence before they get to their weight.

The voice that used to plan the next snack while they were still eating this one, the one that remembered the biscuits in the cupboard at eleven at night, went quiet. People call it **food noise**, and a weekly injection turned it down.

By the end of this page you'll know where that voice lives, which gut hormone muffles it, and the question about muscle that the trials are still answering. It starts with a lizard.

## The two-minute hormone

When food reaches your small intestine, special cells in the gut lining release **GLP-1**, glucagon-like peptide-1, a chain of about thirty amino acids. It is a messenger with three jobs.

It tells the pancreas to release insulin, but only while blood sugar is high, which makes it unusually safe as a sugar-lowering signal. It slows the stomach, so food leaves it more gradually and you stay full longer. And it reaches the brainstem and the hypothalamus, the hunger centre we met in [the melanocortins](/apothecary/melanocortins), with a simple message: we've eaten, stand down.

The problem for anyone hoping to make it a drug: natural GLP-1 lasts about two minutes in your blood. An enzyme called DPP-4 snips it apart almost as soon as it's released. A hormone that brief is a whisper, and medicine needed a shout.

## The Gila monster's gift

The Gila monster is a fat, beaded lizard of the American Southwest that can live on a few big meals a year. Its venom turned out to contain a peptide that mimics GLP-1.

In 1992 John Eng, an endocrinologist at the veterans' hospital in the Bronx, isolated it and named it **exendin-4**. It turns the same lock as human GLP-1, and the DPP-4 enzyme can't cut it. A synthetic copy, **exenatide**, was approved for type 2 diabetes in 2005, injected twice a day.

> The first drug in one of the best-selling families on earth came from the spit of a lizard that hardly ever eats.

Patients on exenatide lost some weight. Drug makers noticed, and the race became one of engineering: how long can you make the message last?

## Longer keys, louder messages

The trick, worked out largely at Novo Nordisk by Lotte Bjerre Knudsen and colleagues, was to bolt a fatty tail onto the peptide. The tail grips albumin, the most common protein in your blood, so the drug rides around protected instead of floating free for the enzymes. Liraglutide lasted a day. **Semaglutide** lasts about a week, which is why Ozempic (for diabetes, 2017) and Wegovy (for weight, 2021) are weekly shots.

Then Eli Lilly built a key that turns two locks. **Tirzepatide** (Mounjaro, Zepbound) activates the GLP-1 receptor and the receptor for GIP, a second gut hormone released after meals. Two hormones saying "we've eaten" seem to be more persuasive than one.

A pill exists too, oral semaglutide, sold as Rybelsus. As we saw in [how peptides work](/apothecary/peptides), the stomach destroys almost all of it, so the tablet needs a chemical escort and a much bigger amount of drug to deliver a little.

## What the big trials found

In **STEP 1**, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 2021, 1,961 adults with obesity and no diabetes got weekly semaglutide or placebo for 68 weeks, alongside diet and exercise advice. The semaglutide group lost 14.9 percent of their body weight on average, the placebo group 2.4 percent. Half the people on the drug lost at least 15 percent.

In **SURMOUNT-1**, published in 2022, 2,539 adults got tirzepatide or placebo for 72 weeks. Weight loss ranged from 15 to 20.9 percent depending on the dose arm, against 3.1 percent on placebo. That is the territory of weight-loss surgery, reached with a pen.

A third trial, SELECT, followed people with heart disease and overweight but no diabetes, and found fewer heart attacks, strokes and cardiovascular deaths on semaglutide. That result turned a weight drug into a heart drug.

## Where the voice goes quiet

Why does it feel like silence instead of willpower? Part of it is mechanical: a slower stomach keeps you fuller. The bigger part is in the brain. GLP-1 receptors sit in the regions that handle hunger and in the reward circuits that make food feel urgent and delicious. Turn those receptors up and the pull of food fades, so eating less stops feeling like a fight.

That has an interesting side effect. Some people report less interest in alcohol and other cravings, and researchers are now testing whether the same drugs can quiet those too. The answers aren't in yet.

The price shows up in the gut. Nausea, diarrhoea, constipation and vomiting are common, especially as the dose climbs. In STEP 1, 4.5 percent of people on semaglutide quit because of them. Gallbladder problems are more frequent, pancreatitis is a rare risk, and the drugs carry a warning because they caused thyroid tumours in rodents, so they are off the table for anyone with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid cancer.

## The muscle question

Now the question from the top. When you lose weight by any method, some of it is fat and some of it is **lean mass**: muscle, but also water, organs and the rest of you that isn't fat. In a body-scan substudy of STEP 1, roughly two-fifths of the weight lost was lean mass. In tirzepatide's scan substudy, it was closer to a quarter.

Is that a crisis? The numbers are in the same range as ordinary dieting and surgery, and lean mass on a scan overstates muscle, because it includes water. But the drugs make it easy to eat very little, including very little protein, and in older people muscle works like a pension: hard to rebuild once spent. Obesity doctors' advice is unglamorous: strength training and enough protein while the weight comes off. Newer drugs are being designed specifically to protect muscle, which tells you the industry takes the question seriously.

The other catch is what happens on stopping. When people in the STEP 1 extension stopped semaglutide, most of the lost weight came back within a year. The effect lasts about as long as the injections do.

## The biggest peptide story of the decade

Put it together: a gut peptide, rebuilt with a lizard's trick and a fatty tail, now produces surgery-sized weight loss and cuts heart attacks, and it's being tested on kidneys, livers and addiction. Demand ran so far ahead of supply for much of 2022 to 2024 that pharmacies compounded their own versions and grey-market sites sold "research" semaglutide in vials, with the purity problems described in the first essay of this series. Three of the people who made it possible, Joel Habener, Svetlana Mojsov and Lotte Bjerre Knudsen, shared a Lasker Award in 2024.

Every other peptide in this series is still knocking on medicine's door, and this one has already moved in.

## Before you try anything

These are prescription drugs for a reason: a doctor should check your blood sugar, kidneys, gallbladder history, thyroid history and the medicines you already take, and watch your weight loss for muscle as well as fat. Skip the vial from a website that says "not for human use". The real thing exists, and so does a pharmacist who can tell you what's in it.
