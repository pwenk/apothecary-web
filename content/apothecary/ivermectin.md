In the 1970s a Japanese chemist walked the edge of a golf course with a small plastic bag. The pinch of dirt he took home held a microbe that would end up in billions of doses, half a Nobel Prize, and the angriest pharmacy queues of the pandemic.

The same molecule paralyses a worm in seconds and leaves the person carrying it almost untouched. By the end of this page you'll know the trick that makes that possible, and why the leap from worms to a virus was always a long one.

## A pinch of dirt from Kawana

Satoshi Ōmura, at the Kitasato Institute in Tokyo, collected soil the way some people collect stamps. One sample came from beside a golf course in Kawana, on the coast of Itō in Shizuoka. His team grew a *Streptomyces* strain out of it and sent it to Merck in New Jersey, under a deal to test Japanese microbes in American animals.

At Merck, William Campbell's group fed the broth to mice infected with intestinal worms. The worms vanished. The active molecules, published in 1979, were named avermectins, and chemists softened one of them into ivermectin.

Ivermectin went first to cattle, sheep, horses and dogs, and became one of the best-selling animal medicines in history. In 1987 Merck took a rarer step and promised to give the human version away free, for as long as needed, to everyone at risk of one particular worm. **A dewormer for cows became the longest-running drug donation in history.** In 2015 Campbell and Ōmura shared half the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine; the other half went to Tu Youyou for the malaria drug artemisinin.

To see why that worm mattered so much, you need to visit a fast river.

## The worm that blinds

River blindness is caused by *Onchocerca volvulus*, a thread-like worm spread by black flies that breed in rushing water. Adult worms coil in lumps under the skin and live for more than a decade, shedding millions of microscopic young. Those young wander through the skin, causing an itch people describe as unbearable, and drift into the eyes, where the inflammation they trigger slowly scars the cornea and the nerve behind it.

For generations, families in parts of West Africa abandoned fertile river valleys because so many adults there went blind. Ivermectin kills the young worms and keeps the adults from making more for months. Given to whole communities once or twice a year for long enough, it can break the cycle.

It has. Colombia, Ecuador, Mexico and Guatemala were verified free of transmission between 2013 and 2016. In January 2025 Niger became the first country in Africa to join them. Ivermectin also treats scabies, head lice and a gut worm called *Strongyloides*.

So how does one pill flatten a parasite and spare its host?

## A lock only invertebrates have

Worm and insect nerves and muscles carry a special gate called a glutamate-gated chloride channel. Ivermectin wedges it open. Chloride floods in, the nerve or muscle can't fire, and the worm goes limp, unable to feed or hold its place.

Mammals don't have that gate. We do have look-alike gates in the brain, but a pump at the blood-brain barrier, P-glycoprotein, throws ivermectin back out before it reaches them. Some collies and related dog breeds are born with a broken copy of that pump, and for them a farm dose can bring tremors, coma or death. **The safety of ivermectin in people depends on a bouncer at the brain's door.**

> A worm and a person can swallow the same pill, and only one of them carries the lock it fits.

Keep that bouncer in mind. In April 2020 a lab in Melbourne reported something that seemed to ignore it.

## The pandemic detour

The Monash University team dropped ivermectin onto monkey kidney cells infected with the COVID virus. Within two days the amount of virus fell about five-thousand-fold. The catch was in the concentration: far higher than a normal human dose reaches in the blood.

What followed was a fever. Small trials, many from places with weak oversight, were pooled into hopeful meta-analyses. One of the most influential, a preprint from Egypt, was withdrawn in July 2021 after a London medical student found duplicated patients and impossible numbers. Researchers noticed another twist: trials from regions where *Strongyloides* is common looked better. Severe COVID was treated with steroids, steroids can let that worm explode through the body, and ivermectin prevents that, so the drug may have been saving people from a parasite.

Then the large, careful trials reported.

- **TOGETHER** (Brazil, NEJM 2022) randomised 1,358 high-risk outpatients to ivermectin or placebo. Hospital or prolonged emergency care: 14.7% versus 16.3%, a difference chance explains easily.
- **ACTIV-6** (United States, JAMA 2022 and 2023) tested a standard dose in 1,591 people and a higher, longer course in 1,206 more. Recovery took the same time with drug or placebo, about eleven days in the second study.
- **PRINCIPLE** (UK, 2024) gave ivermectin to 2,157 people at home. Self-reported recovery came about two days sooner, with no difference in hospital admission, death or how people felt six months later. The authors judged a meaningful benefit unlikely.

By then the queues had moved to farm-supply stores, where people bought paste meant for horses. Poison-control calls in the US rose sharply in the summer of 2021, and the FDA posted a line that went round the world: "You are not a horse. You are not a cow."

## The cancer chatter

The newest wave of interest is cancer. In lab dishes and mice, ivermectin slows some tumour cells, touching growth signals and the cell's chloride balance. Those effects, like the virus result, mostly need concentrations above ordinary human blood levels. A few small early trials test it beside standard treatments, and case stories circulate online.

No controlled trial has shown that ivermectin shrinks a cancer or helps people with cancer live longer. The evidence is lab-deep and thin, and the history above suggests how such stories tend to end.

## Before you try anything

Ivermectin is a prescription drug for parasites, dosed by body weight, and it interacts with blood thinners and with drugs that block the same brain-door pump. Animal products are made for animals and can contain other ingredients. If you're worried about worms, a doctor can test for them, and if you're worried about cancer, every hour belongs to treatment that works.

Ivermectin came from a microbe in Japanese dirt. The next remedy comes from the inner bark of a South American tree with pink flowers, sold as a cure for yeast, cancer and almost everything else. America's National Cancer Institute tested its active molecule in the 1970s, and the trial ran into a problem nobody had expected from a tea.
