Shine ultraviolet light on the bones of someone who has taken a tetracycline antibiotic and the newest layers glow yellow-green. In 1980 archaeologists saw that same glow in skeletons buried in Sudanese Nubia around the year 350.

Nobody in that village had a pharmacy. By the end of this page you'll know where their antibiotic came from, why the modern version sulks at a glass of milk, and why dermatologists prescribe it at doses too small to kill a single germ.

## Gold from a grass plot

In 1945 a soil scientist at the University of Missouri mailed a dozen dirt samples to Lederle Laboratories in New York. One came from Plot 23 of Sanborn Field, a strip of timothy grass left untouched since 1888. Benjamin Duggar, a 73-year-old botanist working for Lederle, grew a golden bacterium out of it and named it *Streptomyces aureofaciens*, "the one that makes gold."

Its yellow product, aureomycin, was published in 1948 and worked against a startling range of bacteria. Pfizer answered in 1950 with Terramycin from another soil microbe. In the early 1950s Lloyd Conover at Pfizer snipped one chlorine atom off aureomycin and got tetracycline, the drug that named the family. Doxycycline, a lab-tuned cousin built from Terramycin, reached patients in the late 1960s and outlived nearly all its relatives.

Lederle also noticed that chicks fed the leftover fermentation mash grew faster. Within a few years antibiotics were going into animal feed by the ton. **Lederle struck gold and then fed it to the chickens**, and the resistance genes that followed are still with us.

The Nubians, it turns out, got there first. Their stored grain grew the same kind of *Streptomyces*, and the antibiotic likely reached them in their beer.

## A spanner in the protein factory

Every bacterium builds its proteins on ribosomes, small machines that read genetic instructions and snap amino acids together in order. Doxycycline wedges into the smaller half of the bacterial ribosome, the part scientists call 30S, and blocks the slot where each new amino acid is delivered. The machine stalls. The bacterium stops growing and your immune system mops up.

Your own ribosomes have a different shape, so they're mostly spared. Your mitochondria are another story. They descend from bacteria swallowed two billion years ago and still use bacterial-style ribosomes, and lab studies show tetracyclines slow them too. Malaria parasites carry a similar relic, a shrunken plant organelle called the apicoplast, which is one reason doxycycline works against malaria at all.

That breadth explains the long list of jobs: chest infections, chlamydia, tick-borne fevers, acne, anthrax, Lyme disease, malaria prevention. Before any of them work, though, the drug has to survive your breakfast.

## Why it sulks at milk

The tetracycline molecule has a claw. Its shape grabs metal ions with two positive charges or more: calcium, magnesium, iron, aluminium, zinc. Once it grabs, the pair forms a clump your gut can't absorb.

Old-fashioned tetracycline lost much of its punch to a glass of milk. Doxycycline is greasier and slips through the gut wall more easily, and its FDA label says food or milk barely changes absorption. Antacids, calcium and magnesium products, iron supplements and bismuth stomach remedies still trap it.

The claw keeps working once the drug is inside you. It grabs calcium wherever new bone is being laid down, which is the yellow glow in those Nubian skeletons, and doctors use the same glow on purpose to time bone growth in biopsies. It grabs calcium in teeth that are still forming, too. Taken in the second half of pregnancy or before about age eight, tetracyclines can stain permanent teeth yellow-grey-brown.

> The claw that grabs calcium in a glass of milk is the same claw that grabs it in a child's tooth.

Doxycycline turns out to be gentler here than its elders. A 2015 CDC study examined 58 children on an Arizona reservation who'd had short courses before age eight for Rocky Mountain spotted fever, and found no visible staining. Short courses for dangerous infections in young children are now accepted. The label's warning still stands for long or repeated use.

## Sunburn and a burning throat

Doxycycline soaks up ultraviolet light in the skin and turns it into chemical damage, so a normal afternoon outdoors can end in a sunburn far out of proportion. In some people the nails even lift from their beds after strong sun.

A capsule that sticks halfway down can also burn an ulcer into the gullet. That is why the label talks about plenty of water and not lying down right away, and why people who skip that advice remember the night they found out.

Both are nuisances. The interesting surprise came from a dentist's lab.

## The second career at a whisper

In 1983 Lorne Golub's team at Stony Brook was studying the gums of diabetic rats, where collagen breaks down fast. They gave minocycline, a tetracycline cousin, and the collagen-eating activity dropped in a way that didn't seem to depend on killing bacteria.

The culprits were matrix metalloproteinases, MMPs for short: the body's collagen scissors, which need zinc and calcium to cut. Tetracyclines grab those metals. **The same claw that hates milk blunts the scissors**, and it does so at blood levels well below what an infection needs.

That idea became two products. In 1998 the FDA approved a sub-antimicrobial doxycycline to go alongside deep cleaning for gum disease. In 2006 a slow-release version followed for the red bumps of rosacea, designed to stay under the level that drives antibiotic resistance. For acne, doctors use ordinary antibiotic doses that both thin out the skin bacterium and calm the inflammation, and try to keep courses short.

The enthusiasm has limits. A 2020 trial in JAMA gave doxycycline to people with small aortic aneurysms, hoping to slow the MMPs that weaken the vessel wall, and it did not slow their growth. A large UK trial during the pandemic, PRINCIPLE, tested it for COVID-19 at home and found no benefit.

The full-strength drug has a few more tricks up its sleeve, mostly for people who travel, hike or date.

## Ticks, mosquitoes and the morning after

Doxycycline is a first choice for early Lyme disease. In places where Lyme is common, a single dose soon after a high-risk tick bite cut the chance of infection in a 2001 NEJM trial.

For travellers it's one of the malaria-prevention options. It kills parasites in the blood, after they've left the liver, which is why the label asks people to keep taking it for four weeks after leaving a malaria zone.

The newest use is in sexual health. In a 2023 NEJM trial among men who have sex with men and transgender women, doxycycline taken within three days after condomless sex cut new cases of chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis by about two-thirds. The same trial saw more tetracycline-resistant gonorrhoea, which is why public-health agencies offer it to selected groups only.

## Before you try anything

Doxycycline is a prescription antibiotic, and that matters: every unnecessary course trains bacteria somewhere. Tell your doctor about pregnancy, children's ages, antacids, iron or calcium supplements, isotretinoin, blood thinners and seizure medicines, and plan for the sun.

Doxycycline came from a grass plot in Missouri. The next drug came from a patch of dirt beside a Japanese golf course. It won a Nobel Prize, freed whole villages from a worm that blinds people, and then became the most fought-over pill of the pandemic, sold out at farm stores as horse paste.
