Somewhere in your bloodstream right now, a molecule nine links long is helping decide how much you trust the person beside you. Another one, fifty-one links long, is deciding whether breakfast goes into your muscles or keeps floating around as sugar.

Swallow either of them and your stomach would tear it apart within the hour.

So why do grey-market websites sell these molecules in tiny glass vials, with a label that says *for research use only, not for human consumption*? By the end of this page you'll know what that label is really for, and who it protects.

## Beads on a string

A peptide is a short chain of amino acids. Your body uses twenty kinds of amino acid, and it snaps them together like beads, each bead clicked to the next by a link chemists call a peptide bond.

Make the chain long enough and it folds into a protein. The FDA draws the line at 40: forty amino acids or fewer is a peptide, more than that is a protein. That rule has a funny side effect. Insulin, with 51 amino acids spread over two chains, counts as a protein in the eyes of the regulator, even though every biology textbook calls it a peptide hormone.

The body is full of these short messages. **Oxytocin** is nine amino acids long. It pushes a baby out, lets the milk down and makes a touch feel warmer than it should. **GLP-1**, the gut hormone behind Ozempic, is about thirty long. Insulin keeps you alive. Pain relief, thirst, appetite and sleep all run partly on peptides.

Biology loves a short message: easy to make, quick to read, quickly destroyed. That last part is about to matter.

## The lock and the key

A peptide does nothing by floating around. It works when it finds its **receptor**, a protein sitting in the wall of a cell like a lock in a door.

The peptide's shape is the key. When it fits, the lock turns, and something inside the cell changes: a muscle cell opens its gates for sugar, a nerve cell fires, a gland lets go of its hormone. The peptide never needs to enter the cell. It just knocks in the right pattern.

Change one bead and the key can stop fitting, or fit a different lock entirely. That is how drug makers work. They take a natural peptide, swap a bead, bolt on a fatty tail, close the chain into a ring, and see which locks it turns now and for how long.

This is also why peptides feel like magic to biohackers: they speak the body's own language, only louder. The catch is getting the message delivered.

## Why your stomach eats them

Your digestive system exists to break proteins into peptides and peptides into single amino acids. Acid and the enzyme pepsin start the job in your stomach. Enzymes from the pancreas finish it in the small intestine. A steak and a vial of hormone look exactly the same to these enzymes: chains of beads waiting to be cut.

> Your stomach can't tell a hormone from a steak.

That is why insulin has been injected ever since 1922, when a fourteen-year-old boy in Toronto, Leonard Thompson, got the first shots. It is why oxytocin goes into a vein in the delivery room, or up the nose as a spray. Under the skin or through the thin lining of the nose, a peptide reaches the blood before any enzyme can cut it.

Pills do exist, and they show how hard the problem is. Oral semaglutide, sold as Rybelsus since 2019, comes packed with an absorption helper called SNAC that shields it for a moment in the stomach and coaxes it across the stomach wall. Even with that help, well under one percent of each tablet reaches your blood. The rest is dinner.

One footnote will come back next time: the very smallest peptides, only two or three beads long, play by different rules at the gut wall.

## The grey market and its little label

Now the vials. Hundreds of websites sell peptides like BPC-157, Melanotan II and copies of semaglutide as freeze-dried powder, ready to be mixed with water and injected. Nearly all of them print the same line: *research use only, not for human consumption*.

That line is a legal shield, and a very flimsy one. A product sold to be put into people is a drug, and a drug needs approval, a clean factory and inspections. A powder sold "for the lab" claims to be a chemical reagent, so the seller hopes the drug rules won't apply. Everyone involved knows where the powder ends up, which is why the same sites sell syringes and bacteriostatic water next to it. The label protects the seller and leaves the person holding the syringe on their own.

What is actually in those vials? In 2024 a team led by Amir Reza Ashraf and Tim Mackey ordered semaglutide from unregulated online sellers and sent what arrived to a lab. Half of the orders never came at all. In the three vials that did, the semaglutide was only 7.7 to 14.4 percent pure, against the 99 percent on the label, the amount of drug was roughly a third higher than stated, and every vial carried **endotoxin**, fragments of bacterial walls that can cause fever and, in large amounts, shock. Three vials is a tiny sample. It is also three for three.

Injecting is the riskier route for exactly the reason it works. It skips the gut, and the gut was your body's first line of defence against whatever else came in the package.

## The FDA's list, and how it moved

There is a legal middle ground between the grey market and a fully approved drug: **compounding**. A licensed pharmacy mixes a medicine for one patient from a raw ingredient. Which raw ingredients are allowed is the FDA's call, and in September 2023 the agency put a group of popular peptides, BPC-157, Epitalon, Melanotan II and TB-500 among them, on its "Category 2" list of substances that raise significant safety risks. The reasons it gave: possible immune reactions to the injected peptide, impurities that are hard to characterise, and little or no human safety data.

Then the politics changed. In April 2026, after the people who had nominated these peptides withdrew their nominations, the FDA took twelve of them off Category 2 and booked them for review by its outside advisers. On 23 July 2026 the Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee voted 8 to 6, with one abstention, to recommend BPC-157 for the list pharmacies may compound from, even though FDA staff scientists had pointed to a lack of good evidence. At the time of writing the agency had not yet turned that vote into a rule, and its own web page still says it lacks the information to know whether BPC-157 would harm people.

Even a pharmacy-made peptide, then, would be a carefully mixed version of a molecule nobody has properly tested in humans. Keep that sentence in your pocket for the rest of this series.

## Before you try anything

A peptide that turns a real lock in your body is a real drug, whatever the website calls it. If one tempts you, take the idea to a doctor who will check your bloodwork, your blood pressure and the medicines you already use. Ask where the product comes from and whether anyone has tested that batch. A pharmacy can answer that question. A vial with a research label can't.

Next comes the smallest peptide of all, only three beads long, sold under the name of a Soviet colonel who believed it could reach inside your cells and wind back the clock on your genes. You may have heard him called "Covinson". The spelling, and the story, are stranger than that.
