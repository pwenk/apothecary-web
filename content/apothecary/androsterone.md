Two molecules in your body have names one letter apart. One is a quiet testosterone leftover that, in mice, calms overexcited brain cells. The other is the scent a boar breathes on a sow to make her stand still for him.

Supplement sellers, forum posters and even some researchers mix them up. By the end of this page you'll be able to tell them apart at a glance, and you'll know how little of the hype about the first one has been tested in people.

## Fifteen thousand litres for a pinch of crystals

In 1931 the German chemist Adolf Butenandt and his colleague Kurt Tscherning set out to find the male hormone. Their raw material was men's urine, by most accounts around fifteen thousand litres of it, reportedly much of it from Berlin police barracks. The reward was a few milligrams of crystals that made a capon's comb grow. They named it androsterone.

It wasn't the main male hormone; testosterone was isolated four years later in Amsterdam. Androsterone turned out to be what the body makes on the way to throwing androgens away. Testosterone and its stronger cousin DHT are broken down in the liver and skin, and androsterone is one of the main end products, wrapped in sugar or sulfate and sent out in urine. Butenandt shared the 1939 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his work on sex hormones.

**The first male hormone ever bottled was really the body's exhaust.** For decades labs mostly used it as a measure: how much androgen someone was clearing.

Then researchers looked at its shape again and saw it had a second job.

## A steroid that talks to the brain's brake

Androsterone carries a hydroxyl group in a particular position, the 3-alpha spot, which it shares with a famous brain steroid called allopregnanolone. Molecules with that feature can attach to GABA-A receptors, the brain's main brake, the same receptors that alcohol, benzodiazepines and many sleep drugs act on. They don't open the brake themselves. They make it respond more strongly to the brain's own GABA.

In 2005 Michael Rogawski's group injected androsterone into mice and found it protected them from several kinds of experimental seizures, in a dose-dependent way. The authors called it low in potency but high in abundance, so it may help set how excitable a brain is. That makes androsterone a neurosteroid: a steroid that acts directly on nerve cells.

> Androsterone was discovered as a male hormone and may matter more as a brake pedal.

Nobody has shown what that means for mood or anxiety in a living person. That gap is where the marketing moves in, and where the name confusion starts.

## Three names, three molecules

**Androsterone** is the testosterone leftover above: two oxygens, a weak androgen, a neurosteroid in mice.

**Androstenone** is a different steroid with one oxygen and an extra double bond. Boars make it in their testes and release it in saliva. When a sow in heat smells it, she freezes in the mating stance, and pig farmers buy it in a spray can for exactly that reason. Truffles contain it too, one theory for why pigs are such keen truffle hunters. Humans can't agree on what it smells like: in a 2007 Nature study, people with different versions of the smell receptor OR7D4 described it as sweaty urine, sweet vanilla or nothing at all.

**Androstadienone** is a third steroid found in male sweat and sold as a "human pheromone." Small studies report shifts in women's mood, attention or ratings of men after sniffing it, including one speed-dating experiment; other studies found little or nothing. No human pheromone has been firmly identified.

The confusion has a real-world cost. "Pheromone" colognes often contain androstenone, and androsterone oils get bought in the belief that they'll smell like sex. **One letter separates a brain steroid from a barnyard spray.** Jamal Dinkoui, the Berbarian Wizard, opens his guide to pheromones with exactly this warning: androsterone and androstenone are different chemicals.

Which leaves the people rubbing androsterone onto their skin.

## What the skin-oil crowd claims

In the circles influenced by Ray Peat, androsterone is sold as a topical oil, notably by Georgi Dinkov's company, and credited with a long list of effects: blocking estrogen production, shrinking puffy male breast tissue, sparing or helping scalp hair, supporting thyroid function, lowering prolactin and lifting mood.

The most specific claim has a lab root. In 1984 Perel and colleagues showed that androsterone, DHT and a related steroid each cut aromatase activity, the enzyme that turns androgens into estrogen, by more than 90 percent in cultured human breast-cancer cells. Dinkov built on that and reported a fall in measured estrogen during androsterone-only use.

Dinkoui handles this material well. He records Dinkov's success claims, his own favourable experience, and also a forum user who saw no lasting breast change after about 18 months of topical use, apart from local hair growth. He notes that Dinkov sells the product being discussed. And he concludes that the human evidence is anecdote, with weak diagnosis and unverified products in every direction.

**What holds up.** Androsterone is a real neurosteroid in animal studies and a real aromatase inhibitor in cell cultures. **What doesn't.** No controlled human trial has tested it for hair, thyroid, prolactin, breast tissue or mood. Nobody has published how much a skin oil puts into the blood, or what that does to the body's own hormone production. Evidence: thin.

## The legal grey zone

In the United States, androsterone isn't named on the statutory list of controlled anabolic steroids, which is how it ends up sold online. That doesn't make it an approved drug or a lawful, reviewed supplement: nobody checks purity or dose, and labels on steroid products have often been wrong. Anti-doping rules treat androsterone and other testosterone metabolites as banned when taken from outside, so athletes who get tested should stay away.

## Before you try anything

Applying a steroid to your skin is hormone therapy, whatever the bottle calls it. Get baseline bloodwork, including testosterone, estradiol, prolactin and thyroid, and tell a doctor, especially if you are trying to conceive, have breast or prostate concerns, or take sedatives that act on the same brain brake.

Androsterone leans on the brain's brake. The next molecule is the accelerator, famous as the pleasure chemical. In the essay on cabergoline it was the hand on prolactin's leash. It also makes you want, and it fires hardest just before a reward arrives. Next: dopamine.
