Roughly nine-tenths of the molecule sold to you as the happiness chemical never touches your brain. It sits in the lining of your gut, telling your bowels when to squeeze.

By the end of this page you'll know where the "low serotonin" story came from, why a 2022 review set psychiatry on fire, what the pills do to your sex life, and why one contrarian biologist called serotonin a stress signal.

First, the gut. That's where the molecule was found, twice, under two names.

## Born in the gut, named in the blood

In the 1930s the Italian pharmacologist Vittorio Erspamer found a substance in gut cells that made intestinal muscle contract. He called it enteramine. In 1948 Maurice Rapport, Arda Green and Irvine Page at the Cleveland Clinic pulled a chemical out of blood serum that tightened blood vessels. They named it serotonin, from serum and tone. It turned out to be the same molecule. In 1953 Betty Twarog, working with Page, found it in the mammalian brain, and the mood story began.

The body's bookkeeping still favours the gut. Most serotonin, around 90 to 95 percent, is made by enterochromaffin cells in the intestinal lining. It sets the rhythm of digestion, and platelets soak it up and carry it around the blood. Serotonin can't cross into the brain, so the brain makes its own small supply with a separate enzyme.

Your gut bacteria have a say. In 2015 Elaine Hsiao's team at Caltech showed that spore-forming bacteria from mouse and human guts push the gut lining to make more serotonin, enough to change bowel movement and blood clotting in mice.

So the "happy chemical" is mostly a digestive one. The brain's small share comes from the same recipe.

## The recipe, with a night shift

Serotonin starts as tryptophan, an amino acid you have to eat. The enzyme tryptophan hydroxylase turns it into 5-HTP. Then the same B6-powered enzyme that makes [dopamine](/apothecary/dopamine) finishes the job: 5-HTP becomes serotonin, chemists' 5-HT.

At night the pineal gland, a pea-sized lump deep in the brain, takes serotonin two steps further into melatonin. Darkness switches the conversion on and light switches it off. **Your sleep hormone is serotonin in its nightclothes.**

That's the chemistry. The marketing came later.

## The low-serotonin story

In the 1950s and 60s doctors noticed that drugs which drained the brain's monoamines (serotonin, dopamine, noradrenaline) seemed to flatten some patients, and drugs that raised them lifted others. A tidy theory followed: depression is a shortage. When the FDA approved fluoxetine, sold as Prozac, at the end of 1987, the shortage became an ad campaign. Patients heard "chemical imbalance" for thirty years.

In July 2022 Joanna Moncrieff, a psychiatrist at University College London, and five colleagues published an umbrella review, a review of reviews, in *Molecular Psychiatry*. They pooled 17 studies across the main lines of evidence:

- Serotonin's breakdown product in body fluids: no link with depression.
- Draining tryptophan in healthy volunteers: no effect in most people.
- The serotonin-transporter gene, in studies of 115,257 and 43,165 people: no link with depression, with or without stress.

Their conclusion: no consistent support for the idea that depression is caused by low serotonin. They added that long-term antidepressant use may lower serotonin itself. The paper went viral.

In 2023 Sameer Jauhar of King's College London and 34 other researchers answered with "A leaky umbrella has little value." They accused the review of selective reporting and over-simplification, and pointed to tryptophan-depletion and brain-imaging studies that do implicate the serotonin system. Both camps declared interests: Moncrieff co-chairs the Critical Psychiatry Network and writes books on psychiatric drugs, and several critics have pharmaceutical ties.

Where it lands: the cartoon version, too little serotonin causes sadness, is dead. Serotonin still matters for mood and for how the drugs work. And the drugs do work, modestly. In 2018 Andrea Cipriani's network meta-analysis in *The Lancet* compared 21 antidepressants across 522 trials, and every one beat placebo on average, by margins far smaller than the ads suggested.

The bigger surprise is what serotonin does below the waist.

## The price in bed

SSRIs, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, keep serotonin hanging around in the synapse longer. In the bedroom that means delayed or absent orgasm, lower desire and sometimes genital numbness. Serotonin brakes dopamine, the wanting chemical, and pushes up prolactin, the hormone that says "enough".

In a Spanish study of 1,022 outpatients whose sex lives had been normal before treatment, Angel Montejo found sexual problems in 59 percent overall. On SSRIs and venlafaxine the rate ran from 58 to 73 percent, highest with citalopram and paroxetine. Drugs that block the 5-HT2 serotonin receptor instead, like mirtazapine and nefazodone, scored far lower.

The effect is so dependable that dapoxetine, a short-acting SSRI, is approved in many countries to treat premature ejaculation. One man's side effect is another man's prescription.

Then there's PSSD, post-SSRI sexual dysfunction: numbness, absent libido or pleasureless orgasm that lasts after the drug is stopped. In May 2019 the European Medicines Agency's safety committee recommended that SSRI and SNRI labels say there have been reports of long-lasting sexual dysfunction that continued after stopping. In 2022 David Healy and an international panel published diagnostic criteria for it. Nobody knows how common it is. Evidence on frequency is thin, and the label change is real.

> Serotonin's job in sex is to apply the brake, and the pills press it all day.

One biologist went further and said the brake was the whole point.

## Ray Peat's heresy

Ray Peat, the Oregon biologist who died in 2022, built a school of thought around energy metabolism (more in [the Ray Peat school](/schools/ray-peat)). In his essay "Serotonin, depression, and aggression: the problem of brain energy" he argues that serotonin is mainly a signal of stress and inflammation, released from platelets and mast cells and tied to swelling, airway tightening and shock. In his words, it "is a mediator of stress that has to be systematically balanced". He claims it lowers cells' energy output the way he says estrogen does, and he read SSRIs as tranquillisers more than cures. His followers favour sugar, thyroid support and anti-serotonin drugs such as cyproheptadine.

Jamal Dinkoui, the Berbarian Wizard, leans Peat in worldview and once treated serotonin as broadly suppressive. In his later *Testosterone Kabbalah* he narrows that down. Gut serotonin affects motility, pain and inflammation. The blood-brain barrier separates gut and brain serotonin. The receptor and tissue decide what it does. A rat-cell experiment where serotonin cut testosterone production didn't test living men, and he writes that a generic anti-serotonin drug plan has no place in his approach.

## What holds up and what doesn't

- **Holds up (strong):** the gut makes most serotonin. It drives gut movement, nausea (drugs that block its 5-HT3 receptor, like ondansetron, stop chemotherapy vomiting) and platelet clotting. Serotonin-secreting tumours cause flushing, diarrhoea and heart-valve damage. SSRIs commonly impair sex.
- **Holds up (moderate):** serotonin does help signal stress and inflammation in the body's tissues.
- **Fringe:** that brain serotonin is generally harmful, or that blocking it lifts mood or metabolism in healthy people. No trials show this.
- **Dead:** depression as a simple serotonin shortage.

## Before you try anything

Never stop an antidepressant abruptly. Withdrawal can be rough, so taper with the prescriber. Stacking serotonergic things (SSRIs, 5-HTP, St John's wort, tramadol, triptans, MDMA, methylene blue) can cause serotonin syndrome, which can kill. Cyproheptadine is a sedating prescription drug, not a wellness snack.

Serotonin brakes desire at the moment of climax. Right then a second hormone surges, the one in every delivery room and every headline about "the love hormone". Its reputation is better than its data, and one of its tricks is making you warmer to your own tribe and colder to everyone else. Next: oxytocin.
