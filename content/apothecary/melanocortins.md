In 1996, three healthy men in Tucson, Arizona, got a series of small injections designed to give them a tan without the sun.

It worked: two of them darkened on the face, upper body and buttocks. They also started yawning and stretching, the way a cat does when it wakes, and then they got erections that lasted between one and five hours. Nobody had injected anything near their groin.

By the end of this page you'll know which lock in the brain that drug turned, why its cousin became an FDA-approved desire drug for women, and why the original is still sold in vials marked *not for human use*.

## A tan to fight cancer

The story starts with a hormone you make yourself: **alpha-MSH**, alpha-melanocyte-stimulating hormone, a peptide that tells pigment cells in your skin to make melanin. Sunlight raises it. That's your tan.

In the 1980s, three researchers at the University of Arizona, the chemist Victor Hruby, the pharmacologist Robert Dorr and the biologist Mac Hadley, set out to build a sturdier version. Their goal was noble: tan people before they went into the sun, and you might protect them from skin cancer. The first version, **Melanotan I**, reached human trials, with early results in *JAMA* in 1991.

Then they made a smaller, ring-shaped version, seven amino acids closed into a loop so enzymes struggle to cut it. They called it **Melanotan II** and described it as "superpotent". The three men in 1996 were its first human test. The tan was the plan. The erections were a gift from a different receptor.

## Five locks, one family

Alpha-MSH and its relatives open a family of five locks called **melanocortin receptors**, MC1R to MC5R. Two of them carry this whole story.

**MC1R** sits on the pigment cells in your skin. Turn it and you tan, and moles and freckles darken too.

**MC4R** sits in the brain, especially in the hypothalamus, the small region that runs hunger, temperature and sex drive. Turn it and two things happen: appetite drops, and desire rises. The yawning and stretching seem to be part of the same circuit firing. An MC4R drug called setmelanotide is now used for rare genetic forms of obesity, which tells you how much of your hunger this one lock controls.

Melanotan II turns almost all of them, which is why it tans you, kills your appetite, makes you flush and can make you sick, all from one shot.

## The erection nobody planned

In 1998, the Arizona team, led by Hunter Wessells, ran a proper test. Ten men with erectile problems that had no physical cause got Melanotan II or a placebo, double-blind, with a machine measuring rigidity. Eight of the ten developed clear erections on the drug. Time spent at high rigidity averaged 38 minutes on Melanotan II and 3 minutes on placebo. The side effects were nausea, yawning and stretching, and less appetite.

What made doctors sit up was *where* it acted. Tadalafil, the drug in Cialis, works on the plumbing: it keeps blood flowing into the penis once arousal has started, and does nothing if you aren't aroused (more in [tadalafil](/apothecary/tadalafil)). Melanotan II started the process from the top, in the brain, with no stimulation needed.

> A drug made to darken skin turned out to reach the place in the brain where wanting starts.

Desire from the brain is a much rarer thing in pharmacy than blood flow, and a company called Palatin Technologies set out to bottle it.

## PT-141 becomes Vyleesi

Palatin worked with **bremelanotide**, code name PT-141, a close relative of Melanotan II that differs by one small chemical group at the end of the chain and is thought to be one of its breakdown products. The first version was a nasal spray, tested in both men and women. Trials were halted around 2007 because blood pressure went up, and the spray was dropped.

Reformulated as an injection under the skin, bremelanotide was approved by the FDA on 21 June 2019 as **Vyleesi**, for premenopausal women with acquired, generalized hypoactive sexual desire disorder: low desire that wasn't always there, isn't tied to one partner or situation, and causes real distress. The label is explicit that it is not for men, not for women after menopause, and not to enhance performance.

The label is honest in other ways too. It says the drug turns MC1R and MC4R most strongly, and that how it improves desire in women is unknown. In the two main trials, 1,267 women over 24 weeks, the desire score (a scale running from 1.2 to 6) rose by about 0.5 to 0.6 points on the drug, against 0.2 on placebo. Real, statistically clear and modest.

The side effects are the melanocortin family signature. Nausea hit 40 percent of women, mostly after the first dose. Flushing hit 20 percent. Blood pressure rose a few points for some hours after each dose. And because MC1R gets turned too, about 1 percent developed darker patches on the face, gums or breasts, a figure that jumped to 38 percent in a study where women took it daily for eight days, and the patches didn't always fade.

## Why Melanotan II stays in the grey

So bremelanotide went through two big trials and an FDA review. Melanotan I went through the same process and became **afamelanotide** (Scenesse), approved in Europe in 2014 and in the US in 2019 for a rare disease in which sunlight causes severe pain. Melanotan II never did. No regulator anywhere has approved it, and it is sold online as "research" powder and, lately, nasal tanning sprays pushed on TikTok.

What users report matches the trials: nausea, flushing, appetite loss, unwanted erections. The worry is the skin. A 2017 review in the *International Journal of Dermatology* collected case reports of existing moles changing and new atypical moles appearing, plus four cases of melanoma arising from moles during or soon after use. Case reports can't prove cause. They are exactly the signal you'd expect from a drug that tells every pigment cell to work harder, and British dermatologists have said they watch for unusual orange tans and disordered moles. Add the purity problems of every grey-market vial (see [how peptides work](/apothecary/peptides)), and the UK's medicines regulator has spent more than fifteen years warning people off.

The FDA put Melanotan II on its 2023 list of risky compounding substances, took it off that list in April 2026, and has scheduled it for a later review by its advisers.

## Before you try anything

Low desire has many roots: hormones, medicines (antidepressants are famous for it), sleep, stress and the relationship itself. A doctor can check testosterone, [prolactin](/apothecary/prolactin) and thyroid before anyone reaches for a peptide. If you have high blood pressure, heart disease or a history of melanoma or many moles, the melanocortins are a poor match, whatever the source.

MC4R, remember, also switches off hunger. The next peptide works on hunger from a different direction, and it started in the venom of a lizard that eats only a few times a year.
