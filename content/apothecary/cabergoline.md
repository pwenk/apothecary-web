Somewhere at the base of your skull sits a gland that floods your blood with one hormone the moment you climax. For decades people blamed it for the heavy, satisfied, done-for-now feeling that follows.

There's a drug that muzzles that hormone for days with a single tablet. By the end of this page you'll know its name, where it came from, what it's really for, and whether it actually gives you back the minutes after sex.

## Born from holy fire

In the Middle Ages whole villages went mad, burned and lost fingers and toes to a sickness they called St Anthony's fire. The cause was ergot, a purple-black fungus that grows on rye. Its alkaloids clamp blood vessels shut and scramble the brain.

Chemists spent the twentieth century taming those molecules. In 1938 Albert Hofmann at Sandoz made LSD from one of them. Others became migraine and childbirth drugs. In the early 1980s the Italian company Farmitalia Carlo Erba built cabergoline on the same ergot skeleton, and it reached US pharmacies in 1996.

**The fungus that set medieval Europe on fire ended up as a hormone pill.** To see what it does, meet the hormone.

## Prolactin, the hormone that says enough

Prolactin is made by the pituitary, the pea-sized gland under the brain. Its famous job is milk. After birth it tells the breasts to produce, and it quiets the reproductive system so a new mother doesn't conceive again at once.

It also surges around orgasm, in men and women, and stays up for a while. Studies from the German psychiatrist Tillmann Krüger and colleagues measured that rise, and a later analysis by Stuart Brody and Krüger found it was considerably larger after intercourse than after masturbation. That neat pattern made prolactin the prime suspect for the refractory period, the stretch before another erection or orgasm is possible.

Prolactin has an unusual brake. Most pituitary hormones wait for a signal to go. Prolactin is held back by dopamine flowing down from the brain, pressing on D2 receptors on the cells that make it. Cut the dopamine and prolactin rises.

Cabergoline is a long-acting key for that D2 lock.

## A key that stays in the lock

Cabergoline sits on the D2 receptors of prolactin-making cells and presses the brake hard. Prolactin falls within hours and stays down for days. Its half-life is roughly 63 to 69 hours, which is why it's taken once or twice a week instead of daily.

Its main medical job is high prolactin, most often from a prolactinoma, a benign tumour of the pituitary's prolactin cells. Too much prolactin stops periods, causes milk flow outside of pregnancy, lowers testosterone, and can kill desire and erections. A large tumour can press on the nerves to the eyes.

In a 1994 NEJM trial of 459 women with high prolactin, cabergoline brought prolactin to normal in 186 of 223, against 138 of 236 on the older drug bromocriptine, with fewer side effects. It often shrinks the tumour too. Today NIDDK and endocrine guidelines treat it as the usual first choice.

> Prolactin is the only pituitary hormone that has to be held back, and cabergoline holds it back for days.

The long grip has a cost, and it shows up in the heart.

## The heart valve problem

Cabergoline doesn't only fit dopamine locks. It also switches on a serotonin receptor called 5-HT2B, which sits on the cells of heart valves. Pressed for long enough, those cells multiply and lay down fibrous tissue, and the thin, supple valve leaflets turn stiff and leaky. The diet drug fen-phen damaged valves the same way in the 1990s.

In 2007 two NEJM studies in Parkinson's disease found more leaky valves in people taking cabergoline or pergolide, another ergot drug. Parkinson's patients took far larger daily amounts than prolactin patients do, and the risk clustered there. Pergolide left the US market that year. At the much smaller weekly doses used for prolactinoma, significant valve damage is uncommon, but cases exist, so the label asks for heart scans before and during long treatment.

Dopamine has one more side to it, and this one lives in behaviour.

## When the brake is on dopamine

Dopamine is the brain's wanting signal. Drugs that mimic it can turn wanting up too far. A 2010 study of 3,090 people with Parkinson's disease found impulse-control problems in about one in seven, and people on dopamine agonists had two to three and a half times the odds: gambling, shopping, binge eating, compulsive sex.

The same pattern has been reported in people taking cabergoline for prolactinoma, at much lower doses. **The drug that lowers the "enough" hormone can also weaken a person's sense of enough.** Patients and partners are now asked to watch for it.

Which brings us to the people who take cabergoline with no tumour at all.

## The biohacker's case

On forums and in "optimisation" circles, tiny amounts of cabergoline are used to shorten the refractory period, lift libido and keep the edge after orgasm. The logic is tidy: orgasm raises prolactin, prolactin causes the pause, block prolactin and skip the pause.

**What holds up.** In Krüger's 2003 experiment, ten healthy men took cabergoline or placebo. Prolactin fell, and the men rated their drive, arousal and orgasm a bit higher. Men with genuinely high prolactin, from a tumour or otherwise, often get desire and erections back when it's treated.

**What doesn't.** The 2003 study never measured how soon the men could go again; their second session was at a fixed time. A 2018 trial in thirteen healthy women lowered prolactin with cabergoline and found no better sexual function. And in 2021 a Lisbon team blocked the prolactin surge in male mice and the refractory period didn't shorten; raising prolactin didn't lengthen it either. Cabergoline also touches several dopamine and serotonin receptors, so any effect can't be pinned on prolactin alone.

Jamal Dinkoui, the Berbarian Wizard, draws the same line in his prolactin guide. For a confirmed prolactinoma he calls cabergoline the strongest choice. For normal prolactin he gives it no routine place, and he notes a small study in which men whose prolactin was driven very low had worse metabolic numbers until their dose came down. For the refractory-period use, the evidence is thin.

## Before you try anything

High prolactin is a diagnosis, made with repeat blood tests, a check of thyroid, kidneys and medicines, and sometimes a pituitary scan. Cabergoline is a prescription drug that needs heart and blood-pressure checks, can make you faint on standing, and can change behaviour in ways you notice last. If your desire has dimmed, bloodwork is the first step.

Cabergoline works on wanting. The next pill does the opposite: it can't make you want anything at all. Its older cousin started life as a heart drug. This one earned the nickname "the weekend pill" from a half-life of 17.5 hours, and becomes dangerous next to one common chest-pain spray.
