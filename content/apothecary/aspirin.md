About a trillion tiny cell fragments drift through your blood right now, and not one of them can repair itself. They have no nucleus, so they carry no instructions for building anything new. Whatever happens to them, they keep until they die.

One aspirin reaches them once and changes them for good. That detail made aspirin the most famous heart pill on earth. In 2022 the same detail made doctors take it away from most healthy people.

By the end of this page you'll know why both things are true, and what a bodybuilding writer thinks aspirin quietly does to your metabolism.

## A tree that tasted of fever

In 1763 an English vicar called Edward Stone wrote to the Royal Society about willow bark. He had chewed some on a walk, noticed it was as bitter as the Peruvian bark that treated malaria, and tried the powder on fifty feverish parishioners. It worked.

The bitter part is salicin. Your gut and liver turn it into salicylic acid, which calms pain and fever and also eats at the stomach lining. By the 1870s chemists could make salicylic acid cheaply in bulk, and patients swallowed it by the spoonful and threw it back up.

On 10 August 1897 Felix Hoffmann, a chemist at Bayer, wrote up a clean, stable way to bolt an acetyl group onto salicylic acid. Acetylsalicylic acid was gentler on the way down. Bayer named it Aspirin in 1899: *A* for acetyl, *spir* for *Spiraea*, the meadowsweet plant that also yields salicylic acid.

The credit is still argued over. In 1949 Arthur Eichengrün, Hoffmann's Jewish superior and a survivor of Theresienstadt, said he had directed the work and been written out of the story. Walter Sneader went back through the records for the BMJ in 2000 and found the claim believable. **The drug that ended a billion headaches gave historians one.**

What nobody could say, for the next seventy years, was how it worked.

## Seventy years of working blind

In 1971 John Vane, a pharmacologist in London, ground up guinea-pig lungs and showed that aspirin stops cells making prostaglandins. These are small fatty messengers that cells release to say "this hurts, run hotter, swell here." Vane shared the 1982 Nobel Prize for it with Sune Bergström and Bengt Samuelsson.

The enzyme that builds prostaglandins is cyclooxygenase, COX for short. It comes in two versions. COX-1 is the housekeeper, always on in the stomach, the kidneys and the blood. COX-2 is the emergency crew, switched on where tissue is inflamed.

Most painkillers in this family, ibuprofen included, sit in the enzyme's channel and drift out again a few hours later. Aspirin is ruder. It hands its acetyl group to one building block inside the channel, a serine, and leaves. **The channel stays jammed for the rest of that enzyme's life.** Think of ibuprofen as a car parked across your driveway, and aspirin as a bollard welded into it.

Aspirin itself is gone from your blood in about twenty minutes. The jammed enzymes stay. Most cells shrug, read their DNA and build fresh COX. One kind of cell can't.

## The cell with no nucleus

Platelets are pinched off giant cells in your bone marrow and sent into the blood without a nucleus. Their job is to stick. When a vessel tears, they use COX-1 to make thromboxane, a signal that tells every platelet nearby "pile on here."

Aspirin jams a platelet's COX-1 once and the platelet stays less sticky for the rest of its life, about seven to ten days. The marrow ships out fresh platelets, roughly a tenth of the fleet each day, which is why the effect fades slowly after the last tablet.

The cells lining your vessels also use COX, to make prostacyclin, the opposite signal: "stay smooth, don't clot." Those cells have nuclei, so they rebuild within hours. A small daily dose therefore tilts the whole system toward flow. That is why low-dose aspirin cuts the risk of a second heart attack or stroke in people who have already had one.

> Aspirin touches a platelet once, and the platelet carries the change to its grave.

So why not give it to everyone? For about thirty years, doctors more or less did.

## Why the advice changed

In 1989 the Physicians' Health Study reported that 22,071 American male doctors taking aspirin every other day had fewer first heart attacks. Millions of healthy people started taking a small one with breakfast.

Then came 2018. ASPREE followed 19,114 healthy older adults in Australia and the United States for nearly five years. Aspirin did not reduce heart attacks or strokes. It did raise serious bleeding, and the aspirin group had slightly more deaths, mostly from cancer, a finding the authors themselves treated with caution. Two other large trials that year, ARRIVE and ASCEND, found small benefits that bleeding roughly cancelled out.

The trade-off is built into the mechanism. The stickiness that plugs a torn artery is the same stickiness that seals a stomach ulcer or a leaky vessel in the brain. Turn it down and both get worse. Meanwhile fewer people smoked, and statins and blood-pressure pills had already shrunk the risk aspirin was meant to cover.

In April 2022 the US Preventive Services Task Force advised adults aged 60 and over not to start daily aspirin to prevent a first heart attack or stroke. For people aged 40 to 59 with a higher-than-average risk, it called the benefit small and the choice individual. People who already have heart disease are a separate case, and the advice for them stands.

Doctors now treat aspirin as a heart drug with a price. A different crowd treats it as a metabolic tool.

## The Berbarian Wizard's dial

Jamal Dinkoui, who writes the Berbarian Wizard Substack on drugs, hormones and physique, makes a stranger case. In his fat-loss essays he calls aspirin a mild mitochondrial uncoupler.

Here is the idea. Your mitochondria burn fuel to pump protons uphill, then let them flow back down through a tiny turbine that makes ATP, the cell's spending money. An uncoupler pokes small leaks in the dam, so some protons run back without turning the turbine. The cell burns more fuel for the same work and the difference leaves as heat.

He contrasts aspirin with DNP, an industrial chemical that has killed dieters. DNP, in his words, behaves like a switch, while aspirin behaves like a dial, raising respiration in isolated mitochondria across a wide, forgiving range. He borrows from Ray Peat, who praised aspirin as an anti-stress drug, and adds claims about lower serotonin, softer estrogen signalling and protection for fragile fats. His own report is modest: no sweating, just a body that holds its shape a little better when he eats more.

**What holds up.** Salicylate really does uncouple mitochondria; at poisoning levels that is part of why an overdose brings fever and fast breathing. High doses also change blood sugar. In 2002 a Yale team gave nine people with type 2 diabetes about seven grams a day for two weeks, more than twenty ordinary tablets. Fasting glucose fell by about a quarter and triglycerides halved, through an inflammation switch called IKK-beta, and the authors warned against treating diabetes this way. At the other end, a 2024 Boston trial gave 80 adults with fatty liver a low dose or placebo for six months, and aspirin reduced liver fat. That trial was small and early.

**What doesn't.** No controlled trial shows that aspirin improves fat loss or body shape in healthy people. The range where uncoupling shows up overlaps with ringing ears, stomach bleeds and kidney strain, and ringing ears are the classic first sign of too much salicylate. His own later essays narrow aspirin to a diagnosis or a measured experiment. For the metabolism claim, the evidence is thin.

There is one group for whom the argument is closed, and it took a mystery from Australia to find them.

## Children and Reye's syndrome

In 1963 the Australian pathologist Douglas Reye described children who were getting over flu or chickenpox and then, suddenly, began vomiting, grew confused and slipped toward coma. Their livers filled with fat and their brains swelled. Under the microscope their mitochondria looked damaged, the dark side of the organelle we just met.

Studies in the United States in the early 1980s linked the syndrome to aspirin given during a viral illness. Warnings followed in 1982 and labels in 1986. US cases fell from 555 in 1980 to no more than two a year from 1994. This is why fever medicine for children is paracetamol or ibuprofen. Kawasaki disease is the rare exception, and only under a doctor's care.

## Before you try anything

Aspirin sits beside the chewing gum at the till, yet it changes how you bleed for a week. If it was prescribed after a heart attack, stroke or stent, stopping it suddenly is its own risk, so that conversation belongs with your doctor. Mention ulcers, kidney problems, asthma, blood thinners, fish oil or high-dose vitamin E, and regular drinking, and tell any surgeon or dentist you take it.

Aspirin came from a tree by a river. The next drug came from dirt. In 1945 a 73-year-old botanist named Benjamin Duggar pulled a golden bacterium out of a Missouri grass plot that had gone untouched since 1888. Its grandchild hates milk, can stain a child's teeth for life, and has a second career at doses too small to kill a single germ.
