Within half an hour of waking, before coffee and before you've checked a single message, your blood fills with the stress hormone. Levels jump by roughly half on top of an already rising tide. Nothing went wrong. Your body did it on purpose.

By the end of this page you'll know why the alarm hormone doubles as your alarm clock, what a lifetime of stress really does with it, and why one of the most popular wellness diagnoses describes a disease that doesn't exist.

First, where it's made, and from what.

## The getting-up hormone

Cortisol comes from the adrenal cortex, the outer rind of the two small glands that sit on your kidneys like hats. It's cooked from cholesterol in the same kitchen as [testosterone](/apothecary/testosterone): pregnenolone, then progesterone, then a few more enzyme steps to cortisol.

Its daily rhythm is one of the steadiest in the body. Cortisol bottoms out around midnight, climbs through the early morning hours and peaks shortly after you wake. On top of that comes the cortisol awakening response, an extra surge in the first 30 to 45 minutes after your eyes open. Then it drifts down all day.

The morning dose has jobs to do. Cortisol tells the liver to release and make glucose, keeps blood pressure up when you stand, sharpens alertness and quiets inflammation. **It's the hormone that gets you vertical.**

The awakening surge is fussy to measure. It depends on the exact minute you woke, so in 2022 the experts who write the rules for studying it complained that most researchers still weren't checking wake-up times properly. The journal now makes authors fill in a checklist.

Who gives the order for all this? A chain of three.

## The chain of command

Your hypothalamus releases CRH. CRH tells the pituitary to release ACTH. ACTH tells the adrenal cortex to make cortisol. Cortisol then reports back to both and turns them down. Doctors call the loop the HPA axis: hypothalamus, pituitary, adrenal.

In 1936 the Hungarian-Canadian physician Hans Selye published a one-page letter in *Nature*. Whatever he did to rats (cold, injury, toxins, exhausting exercise), the same three things followed: swollen adrenal glands, a shrunken thymus and stomach ulcers. The body, he argued, had one general response to many kinds of harm. He later borrowed a word from engineering for it: stress.

Cortisol is the chemical voice of that response. It mobilises fuel and blunts inflammation so you can survive the next hour. It does that by pulling resources away from things that can wait: digestion, growth, immunity and reproduction.

Reproduction includes your sex drive. Cortisol and its cousins act on the hypothalamus, the pituitary and the testes, and a stressed body turns the [testosterone](/apothecary/testosterone) signal down. Curiously, sex itself barely touches cortisol. In the 1998 German study that tracked men's hormones through orgasm, cortisol didn't change significantly. Prolactin did.

> Cortisol makes you able to fight the day, and quietly cancels the evening's plans to do it.

What does the hormone look like with the volume turned all the way up? Medicine found out by accident.

## The miracle drug of 1948

In 1948 Philip Hench at the Mayo Clinic gave a woman crippled by rheumatoid arthritis a new adrenal hormone his colleague Edward Kendall had isolated. Within days she could walk again. In 1950 Hench, Kendall and the Swiss chemist Tadeus Reichstein shared the Nobel Prize.

Then doctors saw what high doses do for months. That's why the most common cause of Cushing's syndrome today is medicine: long-term high doses of glucocorticoids like prednisone, prescribed for asthma, arthritis or lupus. When the body does it to itself, the cause is usually a small pituitary tumour making too much ACTH.

The look is distinctive. Weight gathers on the trunk while arms and legs thin out. The face rounds, a fatty hump grows between the shoulders, wide purple stretch marks appear, muscles weaken and skin bruises easily. Blood sugar and blood pressure climb and bones thin. The NIH puts the self-made kind at about 40 to 70 cases per million people.

The opposite is Addison's disease, where the adrenal cortex is destroyed, usually by the immune system, and cortisol runs dry: exhaustion, muscle weakness, weight loss, low blood pressure, salt craving, sometimes darkening skin. Untreated, a crisis can kill. President John F. Kennedy had it, kept quiet, and lived on replacement hormones through his presidency.

These are the real extremes. Most of us live in the messy middle.

## Chronic stress in a normal body

In a healthy person, a hard week shifts the curve rather than breaking it. Sleep loss raises evening cortisol. Long-term stress, depression and night shifts tend to flatten the daily slope: the morning peak blunts and the night-time low stays higher. That loss of the midnight low is so characteristic of real cortisol excess that doctors screen for Cushing's with a late-night saliva sample.

The popular link from stress to belly fat is weaker in ordinary people than in Cushing's, where it's dramatic. A stressful job shifts sleep, appetite, alcohol and movement too, so cortisol rarely acts alone.

Jamal Dinkoui, the Berbarian Wizard, makes the same point in his *Testosterone Kabbalah*: one cortisol value can't map the whole stress system. Sleep apnoea, depression, overtraining, under-eating, thyroid disease and medication each need their own diagnosis. His practical list is unglamorous: fix sleep and wake times, eat enough, train less when performance falls, treat pain and illness, and get time without work demand. He adds a Ray Peat-flavoured claim, drawn from Georgi Dinkov, that salt restriction raises stress hormones, and in his fat-loss writing he describes cortisol-driven water retention that clears after a high-carbohydrate refeed. The salt link has real physiology behind it (restriction raises aldosterone and adrenaline). The refeed "whoosh" is his observation, not a tested finding. For the wider school, see [Peat on stress hormones](/schools/peat-stress-hormones).

Which brings us to the diagnosis everyone's heard of.

## Adrenal fatigue, a name without a disease

In the late 1990s the chiropractor and naturopath James Wilson popularised "adrenal fatigue": the idea that chronic stress wears the adrenals out until they can't make enough cortisol, causing tiredness, brain fog and salt cravings. It sells saliva tests and "adrenal support" supplements.

In 2016 Flavio Cadegiani and Claudio Kater in São Paulo searched every study they could find on it. Their title is the verdict: "Adrenal fatigue does not exist." The studies disagreed with each other, used inconsistent methods, and showed no reliable pattern of low cortisol in tired, stressed people. Endocrine bodies, the Endocrine Society among them, don't recognise it as a condition.

The tiredness is real, and the adrenals are usually the wrong suspect. Burned-out people usually have normal adrenals and some combination of poor sleep, depression, anaemia, thyroid trouble, sleep apnoea or overwork. The risk is practical: "adrenal" supplements sometimes contain actual thyroid or steroid hormone, which can suppress the real HPA axis.

## What holds up and what doesn't

- **Holds up (strong):** the daily rhythm and awakening surge, the HPA chain, cortisol's effects on sugar, blood pressure, immunity and reproduction, Cushing's and Addison's as true disease.
- **Holds up (moderate):** chronic stress and poor sleep flatten the daily curve.
- **Weak:** everyday stress as a direct, cortisol-driven cause of belly fat.
- **Doesn't hold up:** adrenal fatigue.

## Before you try anything

Exhaustion that won't lift deserves a doctor and blood work, not a saliva kit. Real adrenal testing uses timed blood tests and stimulation or suppression tests. Never stop prednisone or other steroids suddenly after weeks of use: your own adrenals have gone to sleep and need time to wake up, and stopping cold can trigger an adrenal crisis.

Cortisol is a steroid, built from cholesterol. Most of the body's other messengers are built from something else: short chains of amino acids called peptides. You've already met one in this series: oxytocin, nine amino acids long. A whole grey market now sells lab-made copies of others, promising healing, tanning and appetite control. Next: peptides.
