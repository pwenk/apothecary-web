In the 1970s America's National Cancer Institute gave cancer patients a pure molecule from the bark of a South American tree. The tumours did not shrink. Something else happened instead, something the tea drinkers had never mentioned, and it is printed in the chemistry of the molecule itself.

By the end of this page you'll know what that was, why it was predictable, and why a cousin of the same molecule is still being chased in cancer labs.

## The bow-stick tree

*Pau d'arco* is Portuguese for "bow stick." The tree's wood is hard and springy, and peoples of the Amazon and the Gran Chaco shaped it into hunting bows. Botanists now call the main species *Handroanthus impetiginosus*, though most labels still use the older name *Tabebuia*. Late in the dry season it drops its leaves and covers itself in pink trumpet flowers.

The medicine is the inner bark, the thin living layer under the rough outside. Herbalists boil it into a tea sold as lapacho or taheebo. In the 1960s Brazilian newspapers carried stories of cancer cures, and by the 1980s health-food shops in North America sold it for yeast infections, flu, and nearly everything else.

**A wood hard enough for weapons became a cure for whatever ailed you.** Chemists wanted to know which molecule was doing the work.

## Lapachol meets the cancer institute

The best-known answer is lapachol, a yellow compound in the wood and bark. Its skeleton is a naphthoquinone: two fused carbon rings with two oxygens attached. In rat experiments in the 1960s, lapachol slowed a fast-growing tumour, and the National Cancer Institute took it into people.

In early trials published in 1974, Block and colleagues gave patients oral lapachol. They could not raise blood levels high enough to expect an anti-cancer effect without causing nausea and vomiting. And the patients' blood clotted more slowly. Their prothrombin time, the standard clotting test, stretched out, and giving vitamin K brought it back. The programme went no further.

Here is the twist the chemistry predicted. Vitamin K is also a naphthoquinone. Your liver uses vitamin K to switch on clotting proteins, then recycles it so it can be used again. In 1984 Preusch and Suttie showed that lapachol blocks the recycling enzymes, the same step that the blood thinner warfarin blocks.

> Lapachol wears vitamin K's face, and the liver lets it into the room where clotting is made.

So the tree's star molecule behaves like a weak, unpredictable anticoagulant. Its chemical cousin turned out to be far more interesting.

## Beta-lapachone and the tumour's own enzyme

Twist lapachol in acid and it folds into beta-lapachone. Many tumours, especially in the lung and pancreas, carry large amounts of an enzyme called NQO1, which normally makes quinones harmless. Beta-lapachone turns that enzyme against its owner.

NQO1 converts beta-lapachone into a form that immediately falls back again, over and over, a little like a hamster wheel that burns fuel and goes nowhere. Each turn spits out reactive oxygen and drains the cell's energy reserves. In lung-cancer cells with high NQO1, a 2007 PNAS study showed the result: a rapid, specific cell death, while low-NQO1 cells were largely spared.

That selectivity made beta-lapachone a drug candidate. Early human trials in the 2000s ran into side effects in red blood cells, and no beta-lapachone drug has been approved. Researchers still build on the idea with newer molecules.

**What happens in a lab dish is a long way from what happens in a teacup**, and the teacup has problems of its own.

## What's really in the tea

Lapachol barely dissolves in water. A boiled bark tea extracts only a little of it, and the amount varies with the species, the part of the tree and the batch. Analyses of commercial products over the years have found little or no lapachol in many of them, and some products have reportedly been made from sawmill wood rather than inner bark.

The antifungal reputation rests mainly on lab work: lapachol, beta-lapachone and bark extracts slow *Candida* and other fungi in dishes. There are no good controlled trials of pau d'arco for yeast infections or thrush in people.

Memorial Sloan Kettering's herb database sums up the human evidence as limited. It notes a small study in which a lapacho-based preparation may have helped mouth sores during radiotherapy for head and neck cancer, and lists nausea, vomiting and discoloured urine as side effects, with anaemia and reproductive harm in animal studies.

## The Berbarian Wizard's footnote

Jamal Dinkoui, the Berbarian Wizard, mentions taheebo in his guide to lowering estrogen. He cites a 2009 study in which a water extract of the inner bark reduced estrogen-receptor activity and growth in breast-cancer cells and switched on two genes involved in breaking estrogen down.

He is careful with it. The extract was poured straight onto cells, nobody measured estrogen in a person, and he says the study establishes neither lower estradiol in people nor a human dose. That is the right reading. Evidence level: thin.

## Before you try anything

Pau d'arco can add to the effect of blood thinners, antiplatelet drugs, aspirin, fish oil and high-dose vitamin E, so tell your doctor and any surgeon. Avoid it in pregnancy; in animals lapachol harmed the foetus. And if cancer is the reason you're reading this, use it, if at all, only beside treatment that works, never instead of it.

Pau d'arco is a plant molecule that wears a vitamin's face. The next drug is one that grew out of a poisonous fungus on rye, the same fungus behind medieval "holy fire" and, much later, LSD. Its modern descendant muzzles one hormone so well that biohackers take it to skip the sleepy, satisfied pause after sex.
