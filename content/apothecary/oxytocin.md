In 2005 a team in Zurich sprayed a hormone up men's noses and the men handed more money to strangers. *Nature* published it, and the hormone became famous overnight as the trust molecule, the cuddle chemical, the love hormone.

Fifteen years later one of the original authors helped run a much bigger version of the same experiment. By the end of this page you'll know what it found, what the hormone really does in bed and in birth, and why it might make you love your own tribe a little too much.

The hormone is oxytocin. Its first job was getting you born.

## Swift birth

In 1906 the English physiologist Henry Dale found that an extract of the back of the pituitary made a pregnant cat's uterus contract. The active ingredient was named oxytocin, Greek for "swift birth". In 1953 the American chemist Vincent du Vigneaud worked out its structure, nine amino acids in a ring with a tail, and built it in the lab. It was the first peptide hormone ever synthesised, and it earned him the 1955 Nobel Prize in chemistry.

Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus and released from the back of the pituitary. In labour it runs a rare loop that feeds itself: the baby's head stretches the cervix, the stretch releases oxytocin, oxytocin tightens the uterus, the uterus pushes harder, more stretch, more oxytocin. Synthetic oxytocin is still how hospitals start or speed up labour, and how they stop bleeding after birth.

After birth it handles delivery of a different kind. [Prolactin](/apothecary/prolactin) makes the milk. Oxytocin squeezes the tiny muscles around the milk sacs and pushes it out. A baby's cry alone can start the flow.

**Oxytocin is a muscle-squeezer first.** Hold that thought for the bedroom.

## The rush at the finish

In 1987 Marie Carmichael and Julian Davidson at Stanford took continuous blood samples from nine men and thirteen women during self-stimulation. Oxytocin climbed during arousal and peaked at orgasm in both sexes. Their explanation was mechanical: the hormone helps drive the rhythmic smooth-muscle contractions of climax, the pelvic floor, the uterus, the ejaculatory ducts.

Later work kept finding the rise, with messy numbers. A 2021 systematic review in *Sexual Medicine* found results varied a lot between studies and methods. Blood oxytocin is hard to measure, and the blood level doesn't tell you what's happening in the brain.

Touch nudges it too. Small studies have found modest rises after massage, and nipple stimulation in breastfeeding women releases it reliably. The body's lesson: skin and squeeze go together.

So far, a hormone of contraction and contact. Then came the voles.

## Monogamy in a mouse-sized body

Prairie voles are small grassland rodents that pair for life, share the nest and raise pups together. Their cousins the montane voles mate and move on. In the 1990s Larry Young, Thomas Insel and their colleagues found the difference sat partly in where the brain keeps its oxytocin receptors. Prairie voles have them packed into reward areas like the nucleus accumbens, the same place [dopamine](/apothecary/dopamine) does its wanting. Block those receptors in a female prairie vole and she mates without forming the preference for her partner. In males, the related hormone vasopressin does more of the work.

The picture is lovely and well replicated in voles: oxytocin plus dopamine in the reward system can glue a specific face to a feeling. The jump to humans took about five minutes.

## The spray that couldn't hold its promise

Michael Kosfeld, Markus Heinrichs, Paul Zak, Urs Fischbacher and Ernst Fehr ran the 2005 Zurich study. Men played an investment game where handing money to a stranger could pay off or be betrayed. Men given intranasal oxytocin trusted more. Zak later wrote a book calling oxytocin *The Moral Molecule*. Sprays went on sale promising charm by the puff.

> The trust spray made headlines in Nature and quietly failed its replication fifteen years later.

The trouble arrived in stages. In 2015 Gideon Nave, Colin Camerer and Michael McCullough reviewed the trust studies and found the effect hard to reproduce. In 2016 Hasse Walum, Irwin Waldman and Larry Young himself ran the numbers on the whole intranasal field: the studies were so small that most published findings were probably false positives. Gareth Leng and Mike Ludwig added that only a tiny fraction of a sniffed dose is likely to reach the brain at all.

Then in 2020 Carolyn Declerck's group, with Ernst Fehr on the author list, ran a large registered replication with more than 95 percent power to find the original effect. Under the conditions closest to the 2005 study, oxytocin had no effect on trust. A 2026 registered report from Maastricht with 211 people, pooled with that 2020 data, found the same: no meaningful effect.

The love hormone, as a spray, is mostly marketing. Blood-level oxytocin in real bodies is another matter, and here it gets darker.

## Warmer to us, colder to them

Carsten De Dreu's lab in Amsterdam gave Dutch men oxytocin or placebo and had them play money games between groups. In 2010 they reported in *Science* that oxytocin raised trust and cooperation inside the man's own group and raised defensive aggression toward a competing group. In 2011 they found oxytocin increased favouritism toward fellow Dutchmen in word-association tasks, and in life-or-death dilemmas made men a little more willing to sacrifice a stranger from another group than one of their own.

These were intranasal studies too, with the same small-sample caveats. They still changed the story. If oxytocin does anything social, it seems to sharpen the line between mine and not mine. One more intranasal study fits that pattern and is worth a smile: in 2012 René Hurlemann's group in Bonn found that men in committed relationships, given oxytocin, stood 10 to 15 centimetres further away from an attractive woman. Single men didn't. A cuddle hormone with a bouncer's instinct.

## What holds up and what doesn't

- **Holds up (strong):** oxytocin drives labour contractions and milk ejection. It rises during arousal and at orgasm. In voles it is essential for pair bonding.
- **Holds up (moderate):** touch and intimacy release it. It plays some role in human bonding and in-group feeling.
- **Doesn't hold up:** intranasal oxytocin as a reliable trust or charm drug. Single-study claims from small spray experiments.

## Before you try anything

Synthetic oxytocin is a prescription drug that can overstimulate the uterus. It belongs in maternity wards, and the trials behind the "trust spray" don't support it. "Oxytocin" sprays sold online are unregulated, so you can't know what's in the bottle. If you want more of the real thing, the evidence points to the usual suspects: touch, warmth, sex, a long hug with someone you're actually bonded to.

Oxytocin glues you to the people you already have. The hunger for the ones you don't have yet runs on an older fuel, cooked from cholesterol in the testes and ovaries, and the most common way men try to top it up quietly switches off their fertility. Next: testosterone.
