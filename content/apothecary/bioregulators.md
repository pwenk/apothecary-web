In the early 1970s, two young military doctors in Leningrad went to a meat factory and came back with calf thymus, the little gland behind the breastbone that trains the immune system and then quietly shrinks with age.

They ground it up, pulled out its smallest peptides and decided they had found a way to talk old organs into acting young again.

Fifty years later, one of those doctors' names is printed on capsules sold on eBay. By the end of this page you'll know what he claimed, what his studies actually showed, and the one question about those capsules that no seller can answer.

## The colonel and the calf glands

His name was Vladimir Khavinson. Say it with a cough at the front: the Russian *kh* is the *ch* in Bach, which is why so many people hear "Covinson".

He was born in 1946 in Cottbus, in what was then Soviet-occupied Germany, and trained at the Kirov Military Medical Academy in Leningrad. With his colleague Morozov he developed a way to isolate short peptides from calf thymus in 1974. The result, a drug called **thymalin**, was registered in the Soviet Union in 1982. A second extract, **epithalamin**, came from the pineal gland, the pea-sized organ deep in the brain that makes melatonin at night.

The early customers wore uniforms. The preparations were approved mainly for the military medical service, and Khavinson said they were used to restore immune function in personnel hit by radiation, toxic exposure and combat trauma. He named Chernobyl clean-up workers and veterans of the Afghan war among them. He rose to colonel of the medical service, founded the St. Petersburg Institute of Bioregulation and Gerontology in 1992, collected an Order of Friendship from Vladimir Putin in 2017, and spent his last decades known in the Russian press as "Putin's gerontologist".

He died in January 2024, aged 77. His name kept selling.

## From ground glands to three beads

Animal extracts are messy: a soup of hundreds of peptides, different in every batch. So the Khavinson school analysed which amino acids dominated each extract and built tiny synthetic versions, two to four amino acids long.

**Epitalon** (also spelled Epithalon) is four beads, Ala-Glu-Asp-Gly, modelled on the pineal extract. **Pinealon** is three, Glu-Asp-Arg, aimed at the brain. **Vilon**, two beads, Lys-Glu, at the thymus. Dozens more followed, one for almost every organ, from blood vessels to the prostate.

Commercially they split into two families you'll see on labels: synthetic short peptides, often sold as "Cytogens", and capsules of organ extract, often sold as "Cytomaxes". Same name on the box, very different contents inside.

Then came the big claim, and it is a seductive one.

## The claim: a key that walks into the nucleus

Most peptides, as we saw in [how peptides work](/apothecary/peptides), knock on a receptor outside the cell and never come in. Khavinson argued his tiny peptides were small enough to slip through the cell wall, into the nucleus, and settle on the DNA itself.

His group's 2021 systematic review in *Molecules* puts it plainly: peptides of two to seven amino acids can enter the nucleus, bind histones and DNA, recognise sequences in the switches that sit in front of genes, and turn those genes up or down. Ageing, in this picture, is a slow drift in which genes are switched on, and the right short peptide nudges each organ back to its younger settings.

> A three-letter word that rewrites the instructions is the most beautiful idea in the whole peptide shop.

The question is how much of it has been seen outside one building in St. Petersburg.

## What the studies showed

The flagship human study, published in 2003 by Khavinson and Morozov, followed 266 older people for six to eight years. Those given thymalin, epithalamin or both reportedly died at rates 1.6 to 2.5 times lower than controls, and a group treated every year for six years at a rate 4.1 times lower. Those are huge numbers. The abstract doesn't say how people were assigned to groups or whether anyone was blinded, and no independent team has repeated the study.

Pinealon's best paper is a 2012 rat study. Pregnant rats were loaded with methionine to raise homocysteine, which harms the developing brain. Pups whose mothers also got Pinealon learned a water maze better, and their nerve cells handled oxidative stress with less damage. Interesting. It is also a rat study, from Khavinson's circle, and the authors themselves explained the effect by less oxidative damage, with no mention of gene switching.

Epitalon has the most intriguing thread. In 2003 Khavinson's lab reported that it switched on **telomerase**, the enzyme that rebuilds the protective caps on chromosomes, in human fetal cells grown in a dish. In 2025 an independent team at Brunel University London, Al-dulaimi and colleagues, reported something similar in normal human cell lines. In breast cancer cell lines, though, Epitalon lengthened telomeres through a different back-door route called ALT. A lever that lengthens the chromosome caps of cancer cells is a lever to handle carefully, and the paper later had to correct its figures. Still, it is the first outside confirmation of anything in the story.

The Russian epidemiologist Vasily Vlasov put the core problem bluntly: these peptides have never been studied in a normal comparative trial. Most of the literature comes from one school, in small journals, often with no randomisation, and the man at the centre also ran the companies that sold the products.

## The gut twist

Here is the part that makes the capsule less silly than it looks. Your gut lining has a transporter, PepT1, built to carry peptides of exactly two or three amino acids across intact, because that's what your meals break down into. A tripeptide like Pinealon is just the right size to get through the door.

What happens after the door is the trouble. Blood and cells are full of enzymes that chop small peptides into single amino acids within minutes. And Glu, Asp and Arg are ordinary amino acids: the kind of three-bead fragment any piece of meat can shed during digestion. If three beads in a row could reprogram your brain, a steak dinner would be doing it every night.

## The eBay question

Which brings us to the listing that says "Khavinson". Berbarian Wizard's reading companion gives the cleanest rule for it: keep four identities apart and never borrow evidence between them.

1. **A defined sequence**, like Glu-Asp-Arg, which a paper can describe.
2. **An organ extract**, a mixture whose contents change with the source.
3. **A research peptide** with its own separate evidence.
4. **A commercial name** on a box, which can change supplier, purity and contents without changing a letter.

The man is gone, and his name is a label anyone can print. A listing can't tell you whether the capsule holds the exact sequence, a gland powder, or rice flour. That is the question no seller can answer: show me this batch, tested by someone who doesn't sell it.

## What holds up, and what doesn't

**Holds up:** short peptides are real signals, the gut absorbs di- and tripeptides intact, and Epitalon's telomerase effect in cells now has one independent confirmation.

**Doesn't hold up yet:** the gene-switching story in living humans, the lifespan numbers, and any capsule's claim to contain what the old papers tested. Berbarian Wizard himself reports good experiences with Epitalon and Pinealon, and keeps both outside his default plan, which is a fair place for them.

## Before you try anything

Take it to a doctor who knows your sleep, your bloodwork and your medicines, and treat any product as unknown until a lab outside the seller has tested it. A tidy story about genes is no substitute for a batch certificate.
