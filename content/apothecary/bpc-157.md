There is a peptide that gym people talk about like liquid tape for tendons. Inject it near the sore elbow, the story goes, and the torn tissue knits itself back together.

In rats, the story is mostly true. In humans, the number of proper trials fits on one hand with fingers to spare.

And the very trick that seems to make it heal so well is also the reason careful doctors flinch when they hear its name. By the end of this page you'll know what that trick is, and why it cuts both ways.

## Born in stomach juice

BPC stands for **Body Protection Compound**. The Croatian pharmacologist Predrag Sikirić and his team at the University of Zagreb described it in 1993: a fifteen-bead fragment, made in the lab, of a protein found in human gastric juice. Gly-Glu-Pro-Pro-Pro-Gly-Lys-Pro-Ala-Asp-Asp-Ala-Gly-Leu-Val.

Notice the three prolines in a row. Proline is a stiff, awkward amino acid that enzymes find hard to cut, and a 2025 review describes BPC-157 as stable in water and in gastric juice. That makes it an odd one among the peptides we met in [how peptides work](/apothecary/peptides): a molecule born in the stomach that the stomach can't easily digest.

For thirty years, his lab fed it to injured rats. What happened next built a cult.

## What it does in a rat

The rat results read like a wish list. Cut Achilles tendons healed faster and stronger. Crushed muscles recovered. Damaged ligaments, broken bones, stomach ulcers and inflamed bowels all improved in one model or another.

A 2025 systematic review in *HSS Journal*, the journal of New York's Hospital for Special Surgery, went looking for every musculoskeletal study. It found 544 articles, of which 36 were usable. Thirty-five were animal or lab studies. One was in humans.

> BPC-157 has healed more rats than any peptide you can buy, and been properly tested in almost no people.

Most of the animal work comes from the same Zagreb circle. That doesn't make it wrong. It does mean the same hands designed, ran and wrote up most of the experiments, which is exactly the situation where independent repetition matters most.

## What it has done in people

Here is the whole human file, more or less.

A retrospective look, published in 2021, at 12 patients with chronic knee pain who had BPC-157 injected into the joint. Seven said their knee felt better for more than six months. No control group, no validated scoring, no way to tell the peptide from hope and time.

A safety trial in 42 healthy volunteers registered in 2015. The results were never submitted.

A 2025 pilot in which two adults received BPC-157 through a vein, and nothing bad happened to either of them.

And for ulcerative colitis, the condition the FDA's advisers were asked to judge it for in 2026, the only trial FDA staff could find was a conference abstract with 46 people, who got the peptide as an enema.

That is a thin file for a molecule this widely injected.

## The double-edged trick

So how is it supposed to heal? In animal studies, BPC-157 tunes the nitric oxide system, which relaxes blood vessels, and it boosts signals for **angiogenesis**, the growth of new blood vessels. Tendons and ligaments heal slowly mostly because they have a poor blood supply. Grow new vessels into the injury and you bring in oxygen, nutrients and repair cells. That is a beautiful mechanism for a torn tendon.

It is also what a tumour needs. A small cancer can't grow beyond a few millimetres until it convinces the body to build blood vessels into it. The 2025 review by Józwiak and colleagues says it plainly: because BPC-157 stimulates vessel growth, it may be the wrong choice for anyone who might have a cancer they don't yet know about. Nobody has tested that worry directly, in either direction, apart from one cancer-wasting model in animals.

The thing that may knit your elbow is the thing a hidden tumour would love to borrow.

## The rule books

Sport made up its mind first. The World Anti-Doping Agency named BPC-157 on its 2022 Prohibited List, in the category for substances approved for human use nowhere in the world. Because no country approves it, an athlete can't even get a medical exemption.

The FDA has been less settled. In 2023 it listed BPC-157 among the compounding substances that raise significant safety risks, citing possible immune reactions, impurities and almost no human safety data. In April 2026, after the nominations were withdrawn, it took the peptide off that list. On 23 July 2026 its outside advisers voted 8 to 6, with one abstention, to let pharmacies compound it, against the advice of FDA staff, who said the evidence was thin and the substance itself is poorly characterised. Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. has called himself a big fan of peptides. At the time of writing the agency had not yet acted on the vote.

## What holds up, and what doesn't

Berbarian Wizard's take is modest and sensible: he places BPC-157 as a possible helper for an irritated gut lining, slow to act, inconsistent, and pointless when digestion is already fine. His reading companion adds a sharp rule: a repair claim for tendons can't be stretched into protection for the liver, kidneys or heart without human studies on those organs.

**Holds up:** consistent healing effects across many rat models, a plausible blood-vessel mechanism, and no obvious toxicity in animals over short periods.

**Doesn't hold up yet:** proof that it heals human tendons or bowels, any long-term human safety data, and any guarantee that a grey-market vial holds pure BPC-157 at all.

## Before you try anything

If you have an injury, get it diagnosed first, because a peptide that dulls the pain can hide a tear that needs a surgeon. Talk to a doctor who knows your cancer screening history, since vessel growth is the open question. If you compete in a tested sport, the answer is already no.
