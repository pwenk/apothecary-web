Seconds after you climax, a gland the size of a pea at the base of your skull opens a tap. What pours out stays high in your blood for the next hour.

For thirty years, researchers called it the reason the party ends: the sleepy, satisfied, done feeling. By the end of this page you'll know the molecule, the one brain chemical that keeps it on a leash, and why the most famous story about it may be wrong.

The molecule is prolactin. Its first job had nothing to do with men at all.

## A milk hormone with a second job

In the early 1930s the American biologist Oscar Riddle injected pituitary extract into pigeons and watched their crops swell and make milk (pigeons feed their chicks a cheesy paste from the throat). He named the active substance prolactin, "for lactation".

In humans it does the same work. Prolactin grows the milk-making cells of the breast in pregnancy and keeps milk coming while a baby suckles. Men make it too, from the same cells in the front of the pituitary.

Here is the odd part. Almost every pituitary hormone waits for the brain to say "go". Prolactin waits for the brain to stop saying "no". **The brake is dopamine**, sent from a small cluster of nerve cells in the hypothalamus down a short stalk to the pituitary. Endocrinologists call this route the tuberoinfundibular pathway, a long name for a short pipe. Dopamine lands on D2 receptors on the prolactin cells and holds them quiet.

Cut or squeeze that stalk and the other pituitary hormones fall, while prolactin rises. Doctors call it the stalk effect. And prolactin keeps its own leash tight: it stimulates the very dopamine cells that hold it down.

So prolactin lives on a leash. Watch what happens when the leash goes slack in bed.

## The surge after the peak

In 1998 Tillmann Krüger, Michael Exton and colleagues in Germany ran a study that sounds like a dare. Ten healthy men sat with a catheter in one arm, blood drawn continuously, and masturbated to orgasm in the lab. Heart rate, blood pressure and noradrenaline spiked and fell. Cortisol, testosterone and the other hormones they measured barely moved. Prolactin rose at orgasm and was still up thirty minutes later.

In 2001 the same group brought in ten couples. Intercourse to orgasm raised prolactin in both men and women, and it stayed high an hour later.

Then a juicier number. Stuart Brody and Krüger pooled three of these lab studies in 2006 and found the prolactin rise after intercourse was about 400 percent larger than after masturbation. They read it as the body's receipt for a more satisfying event. One partner, five times the closing bell. (Jamal Dinkoui points out the catch: different people did the two activities, nine men in one group and ten in the other.)

The story got a star witness in 2002. The Essen team found a healthy man who could climax several times in a row with no pause. Across three orgasms, his prolactin never rose.

A neat theory, then. Orgasm releases prolactin, prolactin shuts desire down, the refractory period follows. Then people started testing it.

## The theory meets the mouse

Krüger's group went further in 2003. Ten men came in five times. On some days they got cabergoline, a drug that mimics dopamine and drops prolactin. On others they got protirelin, a hormone that pushes prolactin up. With prolactin pushed down, the men rated their drive, arousal and orgasm higher. With it pushed up, ratings dipped a little, not enough to count.

But cabergoline also works on dopamine receptors inside the brain, the same ones that make you want things. The drug could have raised desire without prolactin playing any part. A 2018 trial in thirteen women lowered prolactin with cabergoline and found no better sexual function at all.

In 2021 Susana Lima's lab in Lisbon asked mice. They blocked the prolactin surge during sex: the refractory period didn't shorten. They raised prolactin to mimic the surge: the mice mated as usual. The paper's title gives the verdict, "No evidence for prolactin's involvement in the post-ejaculatory refractory period."

> Prolactin was at the scene every time, which is different from pulling the trigger.

So the acute story is open. Prolactin reliably marks the end of sex. Whether it causes the end is unproven, and the best experiment says no, at least in mice. **The chronic story is a different animal**, and there the evidence is strong.

## When the leash snaps

Keep prolactin high for weeks and it starts to suppress GnRH, the hypothalamic pulse that tells the pituitary to release LH, the signal that tells the testes to make testosterone. Testosterone falls. In men that shows up as low desire and weak erections. In women, periods turn irregular or stop, fertility drops and the breasts can leak milk.

The most common cause worth finding is a prolactinoma, a benign pituitary tumour that makes prolactin. It is the most common pituitary tumour, about 40 percent of them, according to the NIH. More often the culprit sits in the medicine cabinet. Anything that blocks dopamine releases the brake:

- antipsychotics such as risperidone (clozapine is the notable exception)
- anti-nausea drugs like metoclopramide and domperidone
- some blood-pressure drugs and opioid painkillers

An underactive thyroid can do it too, because the hormone the brain uses to wake the thyroid also pulls prolactin up. So can kidney disease and shingles on the chest.

Treatment uses dopamine itself, or a drug that imitates it. Cabergoline is preferred over the older bromocriptine because it works better and causes fewer side effects. In most small prolactinomas, prolactin normalises and the tumour shrinks in about four of five patients.

A mild high reading also deserves a second look before anyone panics. Stress at the blood draw, recent sex, a hard workout, a meal or nipple stimulation can all nudge it up for a while. And some labs pick up macroprolactin, a bulky antibody-bound form that looks high on paper and does little in the body.

That's medicine. The internet wanted something else from this hormone.

## Why biohackers want it low

Online, prolactin became the villain of every flat Monday: the "post-nut clarity" chemical, the reason for a lost edge, the thing to crush with pills. Some men use cabergoline off-label, hoping to shorten their recovery between rounds.

Jamal Dinkoui, the Berbarian Wizard, spent a September 2026 essay on roughly 300 papers about what lowers prolactin, and he is more careful than his readers. His ranking puts cabergoline at the top for a symptomatic prolactinoma, and the first move for any other high result is to treat what caused it: thyroid, medication, kidneys. Supplements sit lower. Vitamin B6 has the largest trial in men, but at a dose that can damage nerves. Zinc and ginseng have studies pointing both ways. He also writes plainly that the refractory-period story is too simple.

His personal line goes further. He advises against porn and masturbation, especially long sessions that keep arousal going, and leans on rat studies where repeated ejaculation lowered androgen-receptor staining in the brain. He flags those as rat data and doesn't claim a proven abstinence period. He also takes seriously a model from Georgi Dinkov, in the Ray Peat tradition, where serotonin, estrogen and prolactin push each other up. He uses it to ask better questions about a case and doesn't assume lowering one lowers the others.

## What holds up and what doesn't

- **Holds up (strong):** dopamine is the brake. Chronically high prolactin lowers desire, testosterone and fertility. Drugs that block dopamine raise it. Cabergoline treats prolactinomas well.
- **Holds up (consistent, small studies):** prolactin rises after orgasm in both sexes and stays up for up to an hour.
- **Doesn't hold up yet:** that the post-orgasm pulse causes the refractory period. That lowering normal prolactin improves sex in healthy people. That most supplements reliably lower it in men.

## Before you try anything

Persistent low desire or soft erections earn a blood test: prolactin, testosterone, LH and thyroid, drawn rested and in the morning, with no sex or hard training the day before. Cabergoline is a prescription drug with warnings about heart valves and compulsive behaviour. If an antipsychotic is the cause, the fix goes through the doctor who prescribed it.

One more thing about that leash. Dopamine isn't the only hand on it. Another molecule pulls prolactin up, and the world's most prescribed antidepressants raise it while numbing orgasm for more than half the people who take them. Most of it lives in your gut, and the "happy chemical" story about it is shakier than the ads let on. Next: serotonin.
