In 2005 a skinny music journalist who had always felt hopeless with women published a book about the two years he spent inside a secret society of men who traded seduction techniques like baseball cards. He went in as Neil Strauss of *Rolling Stone* and the *New York Times*. He came out as "Style," one of the most celebrated pickup artists alive.

The book was *The Game*. It became a bestseller and a rulebook for a generation of nervous men. Ten years later Strauss published a sequel that opens in a rehab clinic for sex addicts.

Somewhere between those two books he found the thing his techniques had been covering up. By the end of this page you'll know what it was, which half of the Game still works, and which half was quietly sabotaging the men who used it.

## The secret society

The seduction community lived on internet forums in the early 2000s. Its star teacher was a Canadian magician who called himself Mystery (Erik von Markovik), and he ran weekend workshops where shy men watched him charm strangers and then tried it themselves.

Strauss signed up to write about it and stayed to master it. He and Mystery eventually moved into a shared mansion in the Hollywood Hills they called Project Hollywood, a frat house with a syllabus. In 2007 Mystery got his own reality show, VH1's *The Pickup Artist*. For a few years pickup was everywhere.

**What made it spread was the promise of a system.** If attraction was a lock, the community claimed to sell the combination.

## The toolkit

Peacocking meant wearing something loud, like Mystery's furry top hat and goggles, so that women had an excuse to comment and you had a way in. Openers were pre-written conversation starters, often a story or a question about a friend's dilemma, designed to make talking to a stranger feel casual.

The neg was the most notorious move. It was a backhanded remark, aimed at a beautiful woman, meant to show you weren't dazzled by her. Mystery's method then ran through stages: attract, build comfort and trust, and finally seduce. Women were rated on a scale, "HB8" meaning a hot babe scoring eight out of ten, which tells you most of what you need to know about the room.

Every move had a name. That was the appeal and the trap, because a man reciting a routine can feel brave and still be hiding.

## What actually worked

Strip the costumes off and a few things truly helped.

The first was approaching at all. Most men who struggle with women barely start conversations, and the community made them start hundreds. Fear shrinks with repetition, which is how therapists treat social anxiety: you face the thing in small doses until your body stops treating it as a lion. **The real product was exposure, sold as magic.**

The second was learning that strangers are friendlier than you think. In 2014 Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder asked Chicago commuters to talk to the stranger next to them on the train. People expected it to be awkward. It turned out more pleasant than sitting alone, and the strangers were more willing to talk than anyone predicted.

The third was fun. The men who did well learned to tease, to tell a story with some energy, to lead a conversation somewhere instead of interviewing. A playful man who doesn't need the woman's approval reads as high status, and that's the "from above" frame wearing a nightclub jacket.

Even the neg contained a small truth. It worked, when it did, because it signalled "I'm not desperate." Warm teasing sends the same signal without the insult.

## What was manipulative

The rest aged badly. Scripts trained men to perform a personality they didn't have, so any woman who liked them liked a character. Negs aimed at a woman's insecurities worked by making her feel worse. Ranking women by number turned people into targets.

The darkest corner taught men to wear down a woman's hesitation right before sex. Here the seatbelt line applies, once and plainly: a no, or a pause that means no, is the end of the move, and any technique built to get around it belongs in the bin.

Strauss came to agree. Asked about *The Game* in 2015, he told Slate: "I wouldn't write it now." That year he also described parts of the community as damaged men sharing hateful, distorted views. The man who built the brand had walked out of the building.

What made him leave was a woman, and a failure no routine could fix.

## The truth

*The Truth: An Uncomfortable Book About Relationships* came out in 2015. Strauss had cheated on his girlfriend, Ingrid De La O, and the only way back toward her, as Kirkus put it, ran through a pricey clinic for sex addicts.

He hated parts of it. MEL Magazine described him bristling at a program leader who treated almost any sex outside monogamy as a disease. So he left, tried polyamory, spent time in swinger and kink communities, and went looking for a way to have everything. It didn't make him happy either. By the end he chose monogamy and Ingrid, and they married. The marriage later ended, which is life rather than a twist ending.

His conclusion is the part worth keeping. Speaking to Slate, he said *The Game* was about male insecurity more than sex. The techniques changed men's behaviour and left untouched the beliefs underneath, above all **a fear of being truly seen**. A man can get very good at the first five minutes precisely because he's terrified of the next five years.

> The Game taught men how to get in the door and left many of them standing in the hallway for years.

That's the answer to the opening question. The routines were armour against intimacy. Strauss had built the best armour in the world and then found himself alone inside it.

## What to keep

Keep the approach: talk to people, often, and let the fear wear itself out. Keep the playfulness, the teasing, the willingness to lead and to set the pace. Keep the grooming and the clothes that fit, because they're a courtesy to the person looking at you.

Drop the scripts, the negs and the scorecards. Replace them with the strange, unscripted move that none of the forums sold: say what you actually think, show the parts of yourself you'd normally edit out, and see who stays. The ones who stay are attracted to you, which is the only kind of attraction that survives breakfast.

The pickup crowd studied women like a lock to pick. A few years later a former US Navy behaviour specialist named Chase Hughes went further, cataloguing more than a hundred human gestures, from a flare of the nostrils to the way someone holds their purse, and promising to read anyone in minutes.
