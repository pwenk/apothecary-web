Ask the internet what makes a person attractive and you get jawlines, hip ratios, six-foot minimums and bank balances. All of those turn up in the science. None of them came first.

In 1989 the psychologist David Buss asked 10,047 people, in 37 samples across six continents and five islands, what they wanted in a long-term partner. Men and women, rich countries and poor ones, put the same quality at or near the top. It costs nothing, no dating app lets you filter for it, and by the end of this page you'll know its name and why it outranks a jawline.

First, the body. Your eyes have been running a quiet audit on every face you passed today, and they have opinions.

## The face your brain has already averaged

In 1990 Judith Langlois and Lori Roggman fed photographs of faces into a computer and blended them into composites. People rated the blends as more attractive than almost every real face that went into them. The more faces they mixed in, the prettier the result became.

Why would the average look good? One answer: a face near the middle of the population is a face without obvious developmental accidents, so a brain that likes it picks healthier partners. The duller answer: an average face is easy for your visual system to process, and ease feels like pleasure. Symmetry gets the same pair of stories, and the evidence that symmetry truly signals health is modest.

Then came the twist. In 1994 David Perrett and colleagues averaged only the most attractive faces, then pushed their shape further from the norm. Viewers in Scotland and Japan both preferred the pushed version. **The average gets a face into the club; the faces people rave about sit a little past it**, usually in the direction that makes a face look more like its own sex.

Faces are the opening act. The body below them speaks in ratios.

## Ratios, measured from the neck down

Devendra Singh's 1993 studies made the waist-to-hip ratio famous. Men rated female figures with a waist about 0.7 times the hips as most attractive, and Singh linked the ratio to youth, reproductive hormones and long-term health. A 2019 review by Jeanne Bovet argued that youth may be the strongest part of that signal.

Culture bends the number, and so does the camera angle. Frank Marlowe found that Hadza hunter-gatherers in Tanzania seemed to prefer thicker waists when shown front-view drawings. Shown the same women in profile, where the buttocks count, Hadza men preferred a lower ratio than American men did. The question you ask shapes the answer you get.

Men have their own ratio. Susan Hughes and Gordon Gallup reported in 2003 that men with broad shoulders relative to their hips, like women with lower waist-to-hip ratios, had sex earlier and with more partners. It was a student survey, so read it as a hint rather than a law. Every padded blazer of the 1980s was a man lying, politely, about his skeleton.

Ratios work in silence. Other signals arrive by ear, or from somewhere above your head.

## Deep voices, tall men, wide faces

When David Feinberg's team lowered the pitch of recorded male voices in 2005, women rated them as more attractive. A deeper voice reads as an older, larger, fully grown man, and the body hears that before the mind has an opinion.

Height follows the same logic with a ruler. In a 2013 study of 650 students, Gert Stulp and colleagues found women were happiest with a partner about 21 cm taller than themselves, while men were happiest about 8 cm taller than theirs. The paper's title says it best: "Women want taller men more than men want shorter women."

The width of a man's face, measured cheekbone to cheekbone against the height of his upper face, is the strangest signal of the lot. At speed-dating events in 2014, wider-faced men were picked more often for second dates and rated sexier for short flings, though no better as long-term partners. The effect ran through **perceived dominance**. Yet when Michal Kosinski checked 137,163 people in 2017, face width predicted none of 55 measured personality traits. The wide face advertises a temperament its owner may not have.

That gap between seeming dominant and being trusted runs through the whole field, and it gets its own page in [dominance and prestige](/attraction/dominance-and-prestige). For now, follow the money.

## Money, status and the famous sex difference

Buss's headline was a difference. In 36 of his 37 samples, women rated good financial prospects as more important than men did. In 34 of 37, men rated good looks as more important than women did. In 2020 a team led by Kathryn Walter repeated the survey with 14,399 people in 45 countries and found the same pattern: men leaned toward young, attractive partners, women toward older ones with resources.

Then Paul Eastwick and Eli Finkel took the question to speed dating. Their participants recited the classic differences on paper. In the room, looks and earning prospects stirred men and women about equally, and the ideals people wrote down beforehand failed to predict who actually excited them. **People are unreliable narrators of their own desire.**

Norman Li's team found a smarter way to ask in 2002. They gave people a small budget to "buy" an ideal partner trait by trait. On a tight budget, men spent first on looks and women on status and resources. Both sexes treated two other traits as necessities, bought before anything else.

## The quality both sexes buy first

Those two were kindness and intelligence. They are the answer to the opening question, and Buss's own summary of his 37 cultures lists them among the universal wants, alongside dependability, emotional stability, health and being in love with each other.

Kindness wins because a long relationship is mostly ordinary days. A face is a first impression; the way someone treats you when you are tired, sick or wrong is the next forty years. Our ancestors who chose cruel partners raised fewer children, and the preference stuck.

Humor rides in the same car, with a sex difference that will make you smile. Eric Bressler's team found in 2006 that women most wanted a man who makes them laugh, while men most wanted a woman who laughs at their jokes. Romance, it turns out, is a comedy club with a very forgiving front row.

> Looks open the door in a second. Kindness is what people in 37 cultures said they'd marry.

The relaxed reading of all this: the body traits are a hand you were dealt and can play well. The traits people rank highest are habits. A person at ease, warm without needing anything back, quick enough to tease, wins most rooms they walk into.

A wish list is a photograph, though, and bodies move. In the late 1990s several labs claimed that women's tastes swing across the menstrual month, toward rugged faces and the smell of symmetrical men when conception is likely. Then a strip club in New Mexico produced the most quoted tip jar in science, and the fight began. That detective story is next: [the ovulation shift](/attraction/the-ovulation-shift).
