Pig farmers own a spray can that makes a sow in heat lock into the mating stance. The active ingredient is androstenone, a steroid from boar saliva. Human armpits make it too.

Bottle that for people and you'd own the world. Plenty of companies have tried. By the end of this page you'll know why the bottle never delivered, why some of you can't smell the stuff at all, and what your cologne really does, which turns out to happen mostly behind your own eyes.

We'll start with the molecule that half the room can't agree on.

## A spray can for pigs

Androstenone is a strange guest. Some people find it sweaty or like stale urine, some find it faintly sweet and floral, and some smell nothing at all. In 2007 Andreas Keller, Leslie Vosshall and colleagues traced much of that to one smell receptor, OR7D4. People with different versions of its gene needed different amounts to notice the steroid and disagreed about how nasty it was.

The blindness can be trained away. Jamal Dinkoui's long review of human pheromones, published on his *Berbarian Wizard* Substack in 2026, describes a 2025 study in which 68 people who couldn't pass a smell test for androstenone sniffed it repeatedly for about two months. Afterwards 49 of them could. **Your nose is partly a gene and partly a habit.**

So the famous boar steroid lands differently on every nose. The classic human scent study took a different route, and it began with a dirty laundry basket.

## The sweaty T-shirt

In 1995 the Swiss biologist Claus Wedekind had men wear the same cotton T-shirt for two nights, then asked 49 women to sniff and rate them. He had typed everyone's MHC genes, called HLA in humans: the immune genes that help your body tell "self" from invader.

Women not on the pill rated the shirts of men whose immune genes differed from their own as more pleasant. The logic is lovely. A partner with different immune genes gives your children a broader set of defences, and makes it less likely you've fallen for a cousin. The nose would be doing genetic matchmaking, quietly, one armpit at a time.

It's the kind of finding that gets retold at every dinner party. The question is whether it survived the next thirty years.

## The replication record

Partly. In 2018 Agnieszka Sorokowska's team found that 24 women not using hormonal contraception rated the scent of men who shared more of their immune types as less attractive. The pill users and the men in the same study showed no clear preference.

The wider picture is flatter. In 2020 Jan Havlíček, Jamie Winternitz and Craig Roberts reviewed the field, separating three questions: whose smell people like, how satisfied couples are, and whether real couples carry more different immune genes than chance. Dinkoui's reading of that review is blunt: across studies there was no clear link, and **no reliable rule for choosing a partner by smell**.

So the T-shirt effect looks like a small tug that shows up in some labs and not others. It's real enough to keep studying and too faint to steer anyone's love life. One factor kept showing up in the data, though, and it comes in a blister pack.

## The pill and the nose

In Wedekind's study, the 18 women on the pill leaned the other way, toward men with similar immune genes, although that result was weaker. In 2008 Craig Roberts and colleagues followed 37 women who started taking the pill and 60 who didn't. After starting it, the pill users' preferences shifted toward men with more similar immune genes, compared with the other group.

The worry writes itself: a woman meets a man while on the pill, stops it years later and finds she no longer likes how he smells. It's a good story and a weak prediction. Dinkoui points out that the Roberts group found no general preference for different immune genes before the pill even started, and that a group average can't forecast how one woman will feel about one partner's neck.

The pill story at least starts from a natural smell. The next chapter starts from a lab bottle.

## Putative pheromones

Two steroids have carried the human-pheromone dream for thirty years: androstadienone (AND), found in male sweat, and estratetraenol (EST), found in women's urine during late pregnancy. Scientists call them "putative" pheromones, which is polite for "accused, not convicted."

The zoologist Tristram Wyatt called this period "the lost decades." His complaint was about method. In moths and mice, researchers start from a behaviour, test natural secretions, isolate the active molecule, remove it, add it back and watch the behaviour return. For AND and EST, Wyatt argued, those steps were skipped. Labs simply started testing the two molecules and building theories on top.

The results look like you'd expect. At three speed-dating events in 2008, women who smelled AND rated the men as more attractive, yet their decisions about whom to meet again didn't clearly change. Dinkoui notes the work was funded by Unilever, the company behind the Axe and Lynx sprays. In 2017 Robert Hare and colleagues gave 94 people AND or EST and found no effect on how they judged the gender, attractiveness or likely faithfulness of opposite-sex faces.

Dinkoui's key point, and it's a good one: **a higher rating, a change in arousal and a decision to meet again are three different outcomes.** Most "pheromone works!" headlines measured the first and implied the third. Commercial pheromone products have been tested in a couple of small diary trials, run with the suppliers involved and with secret recipes, so nobody can say which ingredient did what.

In other words, your body odour carries information. Nobody has yet found the love potion inside it.

## What cologne really does

Here the story turns cheerful. In 2009 Craig Roberts's team gave men either a deodorant spray with fragrance or an identical spray without it, without telling anyone which was which. Over a few days, the men with the real fragrance felt more confident and more attractive.

Then women rated the men. In still photos, the two groups looked the same. In short video clips, the fragrance group came across as more attractive. **The women never smelled them.** The scent had changed how the men moved and spoke.

> The best thing cologne does is change the man wearing it.

Fragrance also mixes with skin in a personal way. Pavlína Lenochová and colleagues found in 2012 that a person's own body odour blended with the perfume they had chosen smelled more pleasant than the same body odour blended with a randomly assigned perfume, even when the two perfumes were equally nice on their own. People seem to pick scents that suit their own chemistry, which is why your friend's signature cologne smells wrong on you.

Dinkoui lands in the same place from another angle. Discussing men who report more attention from women after hormone experiments, he notes that a calmer manner or more eye contact could change an encounter with no change in scent at all.

## Before you change anything

If the pill is working for you, a T-shirt study is a poor reason to stop. Contraception choices belong in a conversation with your doctor, who can weigh your health, your cycle and your plans. Wear what you like smelling on yourself, wash your shirts and let the confidence do its quiet work.

Confidence, it turns out, can be sprayed on a little. In 2005 a shy journalist named Neil Strauss tried to manufacture it on an industrial scale, with furry top hats, scripted openers and a house full of men in the Hollywood hills.
