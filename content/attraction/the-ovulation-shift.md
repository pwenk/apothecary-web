For about twenty years, one of the sexiest claims in psychology went like this: for a few days each month, near ovulation, a woman's taste in men quietly changes. She leans toward rugged faces, deep voices and the scent of symmetrical bodies, then drifts back to the gentle guy when the window closes.

It was a beautiful story. It had sweaty T-shirts, computer-morphed faces and a strip club. Then larger studies came looking for it, and the first suspect walked free.

By the end you'll know what really moves across the cycle, because something does.

## The first clues

In 1998 Steven Gangestad and Randy Thornhill, at the University of New Mexico, had men sleep two nights in the same T-shirt. College women then sniffed 41 shirts and rated them. Women near peak fertility preferred the scent of men whose bodies were more symmetrical. Women at low fertility, and women on the pill, showed no preference either way.

A year later Ian Penton-Voak and colleagues reported in *Nature* that women chose more masculine male faces around ovulation, especially when picturing a short-term partner. The theory wrote itself. Masculine and symmetrical men might carry good genes, so women would want them most when conception was likely, and want a kind, investing partner the rest of the month.

**The theory had a hero, a motive and a timeline.** The labs went looking for more evidence, and some of it walked in wearing heels.

## The tip jar

In 2007 Geoffrey Miller, Joshua Tybur and Brent Jordan asked 18 professional lap dancers to log their periods, shifts and tips for 60 days. Across 296 shifts and roughly 5,300 dances, dancers who were cycling naturally earned about $335 per five-hour shift near ovulation, $260 in the luteal phase and $185 during menstruation. Dancers on the pill showed no mid-cycle peak.

The paper's subtitle asked whether this was evidence for human estrus, the "heat" of other mammals. Something about a fertile woman seemed to reach men's wallets without anyone saying a word, perhaps through movement, voice, scent or the dancer's own mood. It remains the most quoted tip jar in science. It was also 18 women, in one city, measured once, and nobody has repeated it at scale.

Small samples make big headlines. The detectives had clues; next they needed a jury.

## The case for

In 2014 Kelly Gildersleeve, Martie Haselton and Melissa Fales pooled 50 studies and 134 effects. They found robust shifts toward cues of "genetic quality" on fertile days, but only when women rated men for short-term attraction. For long-term partners, the shift vanished. They also tested for the usual sins, such as missing small studies and flexible definitions of the fertile window, and concluded those did not explain the result.

The same year a team led by Wendy Wood ran its own meta-analysis of the same literature and found little support. Two careful teams looked at roughly the same pile of evidence and disagreed. **When experts split like that, the pile is usually too small or too messy to decide.**

So someone went and built a much bigger pile.

## The case against

Benedict Jones, Lisa DeBruine and their Glasgow lab ran the largest study yet. They followed 584 women over many weeks, measured their hormones in saliva at each session, and asked them to choose between masculinized and feminized versions of men's faces. The 2018 result: no compelling link between the women's changing hormones and their taste for masculine faces. Starting or stopping the pill didn't move it either.

The masculine faces still won overall, especially for flings. That preference stayed put across the whole month.

In 2021 Julia Stern, Tobias Kordsmeyer and Lars Penke in Göttingen tracked 257 women across a full cycle, confirmed ovulation with hormone tests and asked them to rate photos of men's bodies. Preferences for masculine bodies did not reliably shift. One test out of many showed a weak hint, which is roughly what chance delivers.

The hero of the story, the cycle-driven appetite for rugged men, had an alibi. What survived?

## What actually moves

Desire itself. Ruben Arslan and colleagues collected more than 26,000 diary entries from 1,054 women, preregistering their predictions before looking. Near ovulation, naturally cycling women reported more sexual desire, both for their partners and for other men, and felt more desirable themselves. Women on the pill, who menstruate without ovulating, served as the comparison and showed no such rise.

So the body does turn up the volume mid-cycle. The best current evidence says it raises wanting in general, with no reliable tilt toward a particular type of man. The Göttingen study fits that too: it found weak signs of more attraction to male bodies near ovulation, unrelated to any shift in which bodies were preferred.

> Near ovulation the body turns up the volume on desire and leaves the playlist alone.

The honest verdict, then:

- **Preference shifts toward masculine faces and bodies:** largely failed in large, preregistered studies.
- **Scent of symmetrical men:** an intriguing early result from a few dozen shirts, still waiting for a retest of the size the face studies got.
- **Lap-dancer tips:** a charming single study.
- **Women's sexual desire and felt desirability rising near ovulation:** the part that held.

## Why the case went cold

The early studies were small and often guessed the fertile window from calendar counting. Many tested lots of outcomes and reported the ones that worked. None of that required bad faith; it was how the field worked before preregistration, the practice of writing down your predictions before you collect data. When the methods tightened, the dramatic effects shrank, and the plain one, desire rising when conception is possible, stood up straight.

If you've ever felt your own appetite rise and fall across the month, you were reading the clearest signal the research has found. Your body was ahead of the journals.

One loose end. Women in the Glasgow study liked masculine faces all month long, and more so for short affairs than for long ones. Why would a face that says "I could win a fight" be attractive at all, and why does its charm fade when the question becomes "would you build a life with him"? The answer is that humans have two ways to be high in status, and only one of them ages well. That's next: [dominance and prestige](/attraction/dominance-and-prestige).
