Two men walk into the same party. Within an hour both are standing at the center of the room, and everyone knows where they are.

People keep an eye on the first one the way you keep an eye on a dog that might bite. They drift toward the second one the way you drift toward a fire in winter. Both are high status. Only one of them is still being invited back in five years.

By the end of this page you'll know which is which, why women find the first one exciting for a night and the second one worth a life, and the exact tilt of the head that gives each of them away.

## Two roads up the hill

In 2001 the anthropologist Joseph Henrich and Francisco Gil-White drew a line through human status that most of psychology had blurred. **Dominance** is rank through force or the threat of it. Every primate knows it. The big chimp eats first because the others are afraid.

**Prestige** is rank that other people hand you freely. Henrich's argument was that humans live on learned skill, from tracking game to making fire, so evolution favored people who could spot the most skilled person in the group and copy them. To get close enough to learn, we flatter, defer and pay attention. The skilled person gets status as a kind of tuition fee.

That's why the two feel so different from the inside. You avoid a dominant person's gaze and watch him from the corner of your eye. You seek out a prestigious one, stand near her, and pick up her phrases without noticing.

Both roads lead to the top. They just have very different weather.

## Same summit, different weather

Joey Cheng, Jessica Tracy, Henrich and colleagues tested this in 2013. They put strangers into small groups, gave them a task, and afterwards asked everyone who had influenced the decisions. Both dominant and prestigious people came out on top. Both truly steered the group's choices.

The difference showed up in how people felt about them. The prestigious ones were liked. The dominant ones were not, yet they got their way anyway. In a second study, viewers watching video clips of the groups looked longer at both kinds of leader than at low-status members. The eyes respect power of either flavor.

So dominance works, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. The question for attraction is what it costs, and who pays.

## Why dominance attracts in short bursts

Here's a fact that relaxes a lot of men once they hear it. In a 2010 review the anthropologist David Puts argued that many classic masculine traits, the deep voice, the beard, the muscle, the willingness to fight, were shaped mainly by men competing against other men. Women's choice mattered too, but it came second. **Much of the masculine kit evolved to impress rivals, and women overhear the signal.**

What do women do with it? Jeffrey Snyder, Lee Kirkpatrick and Clark Barrett gave college women a set of written scenarios in 2008. Dominance displayed in athletic competition was attractive. The same dominance used against people in everyday social life was a turn-off. And when women imagined a long-term partner, their appetite for dominance dropped while their appetite for prestige rose.

Lauri Jensen-Campbell and colleagues found something similar back in 1995. Dominance on its own did not make men more attractive. Kindness did. Dominance added a little extra only in men who were already kind. That fits the 2014 speed-dating result on wide, dominant-looking faces from [the first essay](/attraction/what-is-attractive): sexier for a fling, no better as a husband.

The logic is simple once you picture the costs. A dominant man protects you from the world, and then you live with the man the world was afraid of. For one night the thrill wins, and the bill arrives slowly over the following decade.

## Why prestige lasts

Dominance has to be renewed. The moment the threat fades, the deference fades with it, so the dominant person stays on guard and keeps reminding everyone. That's exhausting to live with and exhausting to be.

Prestige compounds instead. It grows from skill, generosity and being good company, and people give it freely because standing near you is good for them. You can't force it out of anyone, which also means you don't lose it by relaxing. You can sleep, joke, admit you're wrong, and still be the person the room leans toward.

> Prestige is the status people hand you because they want to stand near you.

This is the deeper meaning of the "from above" frame. Above is a body state, the posture of someone who has nothing to defend. It borrows a little spine from dominance, the willingness to say no and hold it, and wraps it in the warmth of prestige. It reads as amused, unhurried, generous with attention.

And you can see it, quite literally, in how a person holds their head.

## The tilt that gives them away

In 2020 Zachary Witkower, Jessica Tracy and Joey Cheng ran seven studies, from lab groups to a real political contest, to find out whether the two kinds of status look different. They do. Prestige showed up as the head **tilted slightly upward**, a symmetrical smile and a subtle expansion of the chest. Dominance showed up as the head tilted downward, no smile and grand, space-taking sprawl.

Read that again with a mirror nearby. The prestige posture is open and lit from the front, the face of someone who is enjoying himself and expects you to enjoy him too. The dominance posture is a bull lowering its horns.

Most people who want to seem high status copy the bull, because the movies taught them to. The cheaper and stronger move is the smile with the lifted chin: the look of a man who likes the room and doesn't need it. Leading a dance works the same way. You set the pace and the direction, and your partner feels safe enough to follow, which is something no amount of glaring has ever achieved.

Status also leaves a trail you can't see at all. In a 2025 study described in Jamal Dinkoui's long review of human pheromones, people smelled T-shirts worn by men with different testosterone levels. The high-testosterone shirts smelled more dominant. They didn't smell any sexier.
