Chase Hughes once wrote down what goes through his head in the first three minutes of walking into a hotel bar in San Diego. A woman at a table, he decides, has a history with untrustworthy men, judging by the tension in her lips. A man at the bar has knee or hip trouble, because one shoe heel is worn at a slant. A couple to the right is on a first date, and she likes him: she licked her lips while he looked away.

It reads like a superpower. Some of it is a superpower you can learn. Some of it is a confident story about strangers who will never be asked whether it was true.

By the end of this page you'll know which half to steal for your love life, and the one skill in his catalogue that decades of research say almost nobody has. Hughes himself, interestingly, half agrees.

## Who he is

By his own account, Hughes joined the US Navy in 1998, worked in Naval Expeditionary Combat Command, captained a small assault boat and later built a business teaching behaviour profiling and influence. His best-known book is *The Ellipsis Manual: Analysis and Engineering of Human Behavior* (Evergreen Press; the 2017 edition is the one discussed here). A later book, *Six-Minute X-Ray*, promises rapid profiling of anyone you meet.

The Manual's first half is a field guide. Its Behavioral Table of Elements numbers more than 120 cues, from arm crossing and jaw clenching to "pronoun absence" in speech. It adds a map of 17 human needs, among them appreciation, approval, acceptance, protection and attractiveness, laid out like rooms in a house. The second half is about steering people.

**The first half is mostly about attention. The second half is mostly about control.** Let's take the good part first.

## Authority without noise

Hughes's sharpest list is the one he calls behaviours that kill your authority. It includes appearing rushed, rapid movements, rapid speech, a hurried pace, a fast blink rate, constant fiddling with clothes, touching your face, over-apologising, bragging, fear of being interrupted and behaviour designed to attract attention.

Flip that list and you get a portrait of the man from the [dominance and prestige](/attraction/dominance-and-prestige) essay: slow, unhurried, comfortable with silence, not asking the room for anything. The Manual's authority chapter says the same in a calmer key. Breathing slow and deep. Ears above shoulders, shoulders above hips. Remaining calm and caring, he writes, will change your results even if you do nothing else.

He also makes a point worth taping to the mirror: unfinished business leaks. The undone task, the lie you're carrying, the call you're avoiding, all of it hums in the background and shows up in your body as tiny hurry. His explanation, a metaphor about piano strings vibrating in sympathy, is one he himself admits may be scientifically inaccurate. The observation underneath is solid enough. **People who have nothing to hide move like it.**

That's the "from above" frame translated into posture. Nothing is chasing you, so you don't rush.

## Attention as a gift

The needs map, read kindly, is a listening tool. Everyone you meet is quietly hungry for something: to be appreciated, accepted, admired, protected, found attractive. Notice which one and give it honestly, and the person feels seen.

His essay on Bill Clinton's charisma lands in the same place. People who met Clinton said it felt as if no one else was in the room. Hughes's recipe is mostly to be fully present, put the phone away, and repeat back what someone said so they know it arrived.

The research backs this up, and it's sexier than you'd expect. In 2012 Gurit Birnbaum and Harry Reis had strangers talk about a recent bad experience with an opposite-sex partner. When people felt their partner had been responsive, meaning they felt understood and cared for, they reported more desire to have sex with that partner. The effect was strongest in people who weren't avoidant of closeness. Attention, it turns out, is foreplay with your clothes on.

> Attention is the cheapest luxury you can give someone, and almost nobody gives it.

Eye contact is where he overshoots a little. Hughes puts the normal length of a held gaze in the US at about seven seconds. When Nicola Binetti's team tested 498 visitors at London's Science Museum in 2016, the average comfortable gaze with a filmed face was about 3.3 seconds. Hughes's practical tip survives the correction: a soft, smiling face lets you hold eyes longer without it turning into a stare-down.

## The signals, read honestly

Hughes's article on attraction signals is fun and mixed. Pupils widen with arousal, which is real, though the change is small and a dimmer light changes them far more. He says blink rate falls with interest, that people point their feet toward where they want to go, and that a woman who picks up your sunglasses from the table has opened the door. He reads a flare of the nostrils, while you're talking, as attraction.

Treat these as hunches to check, never as readings. Hughes himself says you must look for more than one clue and watch for changes from a person's normal behaviour. Someone who always fidgets is telling you nothing by fidgeting.

Which brings us to the dark half of the book.

## Where the lens turns into a lever

The Manual's second section describes the 17 needs as loopholes into the private areas of the mind, paired with a chart of human weaknesses to press on. Its chapters include embedded commands, using confusion as a weapon, gaslighting, covert conversational amnesia, shutting off willpower and creating a split-off "part" of someone's personality. The book calls its methods a weapon. Its ethics page says the manual is for entertainment only and adds, "Remember Dr. Frank Olson," the US Army scientist secretly dosed with LSD by the CIA in 1953 who died days later.

That CIA history, the program called MKUltra, was real. The book also retells "Project Monarch" as a CIA mind-control subproject, and that one belongs to conspiracy lore with no support in the declassified record.

For attraction the rule is simple. **If a technique only works when the other person doesn't know you're using it, keep it out of the bedroom.** Desire that has to be engineered covertly has to be guarded covertly, and that's exhausting for everyone.

## The lie-detector question

Now the loop from the start. The skill in his catalogue that research says almost nobody has is reading lies from the body.

In 2006 Charles Bond and Bella DePaulo pooled 206 studies covering 24,483 people trying to spot lies in real time. Average accuracy was 54 percent, a whisker above a coin toss. An earlier review led by DePaulo found that most of the famous nonverbal "tells" are weakly linked to lying or not linked at all.

Hughes, to his credit, writes plainly that no microexpression signals deception, and that even experts usually need video playback to catch microexpressions at all. The bar-room profile at the top of this page is the same art in a different coat: confident, vivid and almost never checked.

So keep the parts that change you. Slow down, breathe low, sit tall, give your full attention as if it were expensive, and hold eyes a beat longer with a warm face. Skip the parts that are meant to change someone else without their knowledge.

Calm attention, an unhurried pace, a body that leads without pushing: there's an old idea for why that combination melts people, and it's older than any Navy manual. It says desire runs on a charge between two poles, the way a current runs between the ends of a battery. That's where the next wing begins: [polarity](/pleasure/polarity).
