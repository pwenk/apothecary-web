You are partly made of soybean oil. When researchers pooled half a century of fat-tissue samples from American adults, they found the share of linoleic acid, the main fat in seed oils, had risen by 136%. Your belly fat today is chemically different from your grandmother's.

Ray Peat thought this was the quiet disaster of the twentieth century. By the end of this page you'll know why he called these oils toxic, what two trials pulled out of old filing cabinets decades later seemed to show, and why the largest reviews in medicine still come down against him.

## The chemistry of going rancid

Fats are chains of carbon. A saturated fat, like the ones in butter and coconut oil, has no double bonds along the chain. It's straight, stiff and stable, which is why coconut oil can sit on a shelf for months.

A polyunsaturated fat, or PUFA, has two or more double bonds. Linoleic acid, the omega-6 fat in soybean, corn, sunflower and safflower oil, has two. The carbon sitting between them holds its hydrogen loosely. Oxygen steals it, the fat turns into a reactive fragment, that fragment attacks the next fat, and a chain reaction runs down the line. Chemists call it **lipid peroxidation**. Your nose calls it rancid.

Painters have used this for centuries. Linseed oil in oil paint dries by reacting with air until it sets hard. Peat loved that image: the same chemistry that hardens a canvas, he argued, goes on slowly in warm, oxygenated human tissue. Modern biology has given him an odd footnote here. Since 2012 cell biologists have studied ferroptosis, a form of cell death driven by iron and the peroxidation of exactly these fats.

## Peat's indictment

Peat's charge sheet, laid out in his essay "Unsaturated vegetable oils: toxic," is long.

He argued that PUFAs are harmful by their very structure, not just because of processing or pesticide residue. That they slow the thyroid at three points: secretion from the gland, transport in the blood, and response in the cell. That they suppress the immune system, pointing to hospital fat emulsions given by drip. That they feed the brown "age pigment" that builds up in old cells. And that because they're stored in your body fat, every stressful day releases them back into your blood. Recall the last essay: stress hormones free fat from storage. In Peat's world, the fat they free is the fat you least want circulating.

He also called the phrase "essential fatty acid" a misnomer. Mainstream nutrition disagrees: the body does need a little linoleic acid, around a percent or two of calories, and deficiency causes skin and growth problems.

Then the history, which he told like a crime story. Soy, linseed and safflower oils were industrial products for paints and varnishes until petroleum undercut them. Pig farmers found corn and soy fattened animals cheaply, which Peat read as an anti-thyroid effect. When farmers tried coconut oil in the 1940s, he wrote, the pigs stayed lean, active and hungry. His own weight drifted from about 185 to 175 pounds after he added coconut oil. "Coconut oil is the least fattening of all the oils," he wrote. Peat wanted seed oils back in the paint shop, where they could dry in peace.

## The anti-thyroid claim, examined

Is there anything behind the thyroid charge? A little, in a dish.

In 1988 researchers at UCLA added fatty acids to rat liver cell nuclei and measured how well T3, the active thyroid hormone, could bind its receptor. **Unsaturated fatty acids blocked the binding**, and linoleic acid was among the strongest. That's the kind of paper Peat built arguments on.

Look closer, though. Oleic acid, the main fat in olive oil, which Peat approved of, also blocked binding. Palmitoleic acid, plentiful in macadamia nuts, was just as strong as linoleic. It's a test-tube result about free fatty acids at high concentrations. No trial has shown that people eating seed oils develop low thyroid function.

## The two lost trials

Now the part that made Peaters feel vindicated.

The Sydney Diet Heart Study ran from 1966 to 1973. It took 458 men aged 30 to 59 who had recently had a coronary event and told half of them to replace animal fats and ordinary margarine with safflower oil and a safflower margarine. The full results sat unanalysed until 2013, when a team led by Christopher Ramsden at the US National Institutes of Health recovered the data. **The safflower group died more often**: 17.6% against 11.8% for all causes, with a similar gap for heart deaths.

The Minnesota Coronary Experiment ran from 1968 to 1973 in six state mental hospitals and a nursing home: 9,423 people aged 20 to 97, fed either a diet high in animal fat or one where corn oil replaced it. Ramsden's group recovered those records in 2016. The corn oil diet lowered cholesterol by 13.8%, against 1% in the control group, and saved no lives. Worse, among participants, each 30 mg/dL drop in cholesterol was linked with a 22% higher risk of death.

Two trials, the textbook diet-heart idea, and the wrong answer both times. You can see why the seed-oil skeptics framed these papers.

The caveats matter, though. Margarines of that era were often hydrogenated and carried trans fats, which are harmful in their own right. In Minnesota, patients moved in and out of the hospitals, and only 2,355 people stayed on their diet for a year or more. These are small or messy trials from another era of food.

## What the bigger pictures say

Zoom out and the picture changes colour.

A 2020 Cochrane review pooled 15 trials with about 56,700 people. Cutting saturated fat **lowered combined cardiovascular events by 17%**, with little or no effect on deaths, and replacing it with polyunsaturated fat or carbohydrate both looked useful. A separate 2018 Cochrane review of omega-6 trials found more omega-6 made little or no difference to deaths, with a possible small drop in heart attacks.

Large observational studies lean further against Peat. A 2014 pooling of 13 cohorts and over 310,000 people found the highest linoleic acid eaters had 15% fewer heart events. In 2019 a consortium measured linoleic acid directly in the blood and tissue of 68,659 people across 30 studies, a harder signal to fake than a food diary. Higher levels went with lower cardiovascular disease and about 22% lower cardiovascular death across the range.

And coconut oil? A 2020 analysis of 16 trials found it raised LDL cholesterol by about 10 mg/dL compared with non-tropical vegetable oils, nudged HDL up a little, and did nothing measurable for weight, blood sugar or inflammation. The lean-pig magic never showed up in people.

> Nobody has ever tested Peat's actual diet against anyone else's. Everything else is circumstantial.

What stays genuinely open: oil that's been heated again and again in a fryer, which does produce oxidised by-products; the fact that most seed oil arrives inside ultra-processed food, so its effects are hard to separate from the crisps around it; and that 136% climb in body fat linoleic acid, which no one has studied over a lifetime.

## Before you try anything

Swapping seed oils for butter and coconut oil will likely raise your LDL. If you have high cholesterol, a family history of early heart disease or existing heart disease, get a lipid panel before and after any big change in your fats, and talk it through with your doctor.

Peat thought seed oils did their deepest damage by pushing up one particular hormone, the one most people link with youth and femininity. He called it a stress hormone. The next page is his strangest cast of villains, estrogen, serotonin and cortisol, and the hormones he trusted instead.
