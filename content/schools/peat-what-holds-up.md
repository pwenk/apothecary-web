Ray Peat said that a few grams of gelatin at bedtime let him sleep about nine hours without waking. It's the least glamorous trick in his whole repertoire, and it has held up better than most of the grand theories.

By the end of this page you'll know which of his big claims survived contact with human data, which ones fell over, and a way of reading any guru that lets you keep the gold without swallowing the gravel.

## How to grade a contrarian

Four questions do most of the work.

Is the mechanism real? Was it shown in people, or only in rats and dishes? Has anyone tested the actual advice, at the amounts people really eat? And what result would the author accept as proof he was wrong?

Peat usually passes the first question, often fails the second and third, and almost never answered the fourth. Picture a mechanic with a superb ear. He hears the right rattle, then names the wrong part with total confidence. You'd still want him listening to your engine. You'd check before paying for the part.

## The scorecard

| Peat's claim | Verdict | The short reason |
|---|---|---|
| Thyroid sets the pace of the whole body | **Holds** | Textbook physiology, and low thyroid really raises cholesterol |
| Many people with "normal" labs need thyroid treatment | Weak | Treating mild cases in older adults helped nobody's symptoms (TRUST, 2017) |
| Temperature and pulse diagnose the thyroid | Weak | Average body temperature has drifted down for other reasons; stress also heats you |
| Carbohydrate keeps T3 up | **Holds** | Zero-carb diets cut T3 sharply; 50 g a day prevented it (1976) |
| Sugar is a clean fuel | Depends | Fine within energy needs; fructose drinks on top of a surplus added belly fat (2009) |
| Seed oils are toxic | Contested | Two recovered trials look bad for them; bigger reviews and blood-level studies look good |
| Coconut oil is the best fat | Doesn't hold | Raises LDL, no effect on weight in trials |
| Estrogen is a stress and aging hormone | Doesn't hold | Hormone therapy protects bone; benefits usually beat risks in younger menopausal women |
| Real progesterone beats synthetic progestins | **Holds** | No breast cancer rise with progesterone in the French E3N cohort, a 69% rise with progestins |
| Progesterone protects the injured brain | Doesn't hold | ProTECT III stopped for futility |
| Serotonin is a villain | Contrarian | The low-serotonin theory of depression is shaky, yet antidepressants beat placebo |

| Gelatin and glycine calm you and help sleep | **Some evidence** | Small human trials, a clear mechanism, a mouse lifespan bump |

## The wins

Start with the bedtime gelatin, since we promised. Gelatin is mostly glycine, the smallest amino acid. In a small Japanese study published in 2007, poor sleepers given glycine before bed rated their sleep better and, in the sleep lab, fell asleep and reached deep sleep faster. A 2012 follow-up found sleep-deprived volunteers felt less fatigued the next day.

Then came the mechanism, in rats, in 2015: glycine acts on the brain's master clock, widens blood vessels in the skin and drops core temperature, the same small cooling that comes before natural sleep. A sweet irony for a man who wanted everyone warmer. And in 2019 the US National Institute on Aging's testing programme found a glycine-rich diet stretched mouse lifespan by 4 to 6% across three separate labs. The caveats: the human trials were tiny, and several were backed by Ajinomoto, which sells glycine. **Some evidence** is the honest grade.

The thyroid wins are bigger. Peat was right that the thyroid sets the body's tempo and that cutting carbohydrate to zero lowers active T3. He was right that synthetic progestins behave differently from the progesterone the body makes. And he was early in doubting that depression is simply a serotonin shortage, a doubt a 2022 umbrella review made respectable.

## The misses

His biggest misses share a shape: a mechanism that looked strong in animals, stretched into a rule for everyone.

Estrogen is the clearest. Lab studies can show estrogen stirring stress pathways, but in women at menopause the North American Menopause Society calls hormone therapy the most effective treatment for hot flushes and a proven protector of bone.

Aspirin is the second. In ASPREE, 19,114 healthy older adults took low-dose aspirin or placebo for nearly five years. The aspirin group had more major bleeding and a slightly higher death rate, mostly from cancer, a result the authors themselves called unexpected. The same 2019 mouse programme that loved glycine found aspirin did nothing for lifespan.

Serotonin sits in between. Turning off serotonin production outside the brain protected mice from obesity in 2015, which sounds like Peat. Yet a 2018 analysis of 522 trials found all 21 antidepressants studied did better than placebo. **"Low serotonin doesn't explain depression" and "serotonin is bad" are different claims**, and only the first has support.

## The open questions

Seed oils remain the honest draw. The recovered Sydney and Minnesota trials are real and embarrassing for the old diet-heart story. The Cochrane reviews and the blood-level studies of tens of thousands of people point the other way for heart disease. Nobody has tested Peat's whole pattern, high sugar, low PUFA, plenty of dairy and saturated fat, against anything.

Sugar depends on the dose of energy around it. In a lean, active body eating to its needs, fruit, juice and honey look perfectly fine. Stacked on a calorie surplus, fructose-heavy drinks push fat into the belly and liver. Peat wrote for the first body. Most of his online readers live in the second.

> The best way to honour a contrarian is to test him.

## Reading a guru generously and skeptically at once

Be generous about the questions. Why are we colder than our great-grandparents? Why did two vegetable-oil trials sit unpublished for decades? Why did everyone accept serotonin as the happiness molecule? Peat asked those before it was fashionable, and the body treated as one connected system is a better starting point than a list of separate diseases.

Be skeptical about the answers. Watch for the leap from rat to breakfast. Watch for the theory that explains every symptom, because a theory that explains everything can never be caught out. Notice when a follower's certainty is greater than the author's. And notice when a claim has been repeated so often that nobody remembers there was never a trial.

In practice: take one claim at a time, look for a human study of the actual advice, and let your own bloodwork, sleep and energy have the final vote. The body is the most honest reviewer you'll ever get.

## Before you try anything

None of this is a plan. Thyroid hormone, progesterone, pregnenolone and aspirin are drugs with real risks; big shifts in sugar or fat change your blood sugar and cholesterol. See a doctor, get the bloodwork, and change one thing at a time so you can tell what did what.

Peat's rival camp says he asked the right question and picked the wrong fuel. Their leader, the neurosurgeon Jack Kruse, says the body's real power source is light: sunrise in your eyes, cold water on your skin, and less of a heavy form of hydrogen called deuterium in your food. Some of his fans think a glass of Peat's orange juice is exactly the wrong thing to drink. Next, meet the man who wants you outside at dawn.
