There is a brain surgeon who will tell you the light over your kitchen sink is making you fat. He'll also tell you to put your face in a bowl of ice water, drink water with less of a heavy hydrogen in it, and live on the ground floor.

Thousands of people do some version of all four. By the end of this page you'll know how a man once questioned by the FBI on a cruise ship became the patron saint of sunrise, and the one word he typed when a stranger asked why he is so insufferable.

## A surgeon at 357 pounds

Jack Kruse is a New York native who studied at St. John's University, took a dental degree first (he signed his early clinic listings "DMD, MD"), then went to medical school at LSU in New Orleans. He trained in maxillofacial surgery at Charity Hospital and in neurosurgery at LSU, and he is board certified by the American Board of Neurological Surgery. Public records list him under his legal name, John J. Kruse.

By 2007 he was a neurosurgeon in Nashville, 6 foot 2 and, by his own account, 357 pounds. A torn meniscus put him in a wheelchair. He says he then read five to six thousand papers in eighteen months, mostly on leptin, the hormone your fat uses to report to your brain, and on light.

He says he lost 77 pounds in three months and 133 in a year. Nobody has audited those numbers, and the story is the founding myth of everything that follows. **A surgeon who fixed his own body by reading** is a hard figure to argue with, and he knows it.

## The 2011 blog that ate the paleo forums

In June 2011 Kruse started blogging. Two ideas made him famous inside the low-carb world within a year.

The first was the **Leptin Rx**, or leptin reset: a big protein-and-fat breakfast soon after waking, no snacks ever, few carbs, and dark evenings. His claim was that this schedule rewires the hypothalamus, the small brain region that sets hunger and body temperature, "without ever using a blade."

The second was **cold thermogenesis**, "CT" to his readers. Start with your face in ice water, graduate to bags of ice on your torso, end in a tub full of it. He promised fat loss, better thyroid function, fertility, deep sleep and reversed diabetes.

His own site claims one paleo forum thread about his ideas logged over 1.5 million views in about a year. The fame came with enemies. In May 2012 he was booked to speak on Jimmy Moore's low-carb cruise out of Galveston when a fake Twitter account in his name posted about carrying "a vial of Legionnaires." Police, the FBI and the Coast Guard boarded the ship, searched his bags and questioned him. They cleared him. The ship sailed without him anyway. He blamed rivals in the diet wars. In 2013 the blog became a book, *The Epi-Paleo Rx*.

So far this is a diet guru with an unusually good villain story. Then he stopped talking about food.

## From breakfast to physics

Read his later "About" page and the diet has nearly disappeared. Obesity, he writes, "begins in the eye because of an altered spectrum of light." Modern disease starts with Edison's light bulb and Tesla's power grid. Food is the passenger; light is driving.

His model goes like this. Your mitochondria, the tiny engines inside each cell, evolved under sunlight. Sunlight (ultraviolet at dawn and noon, infrared all day) tunes them. Artificial blue light at night, phone and Wi-Fi radiation (he coined "nnEMF," non-native electromagnetic fields) and a heavy form of hydrogen called deuterium jam them. Cold, seafood rich in the fat DHA, bare feet on the ground and sunrise in your eyes restore them.

Think of a solar-powered house wired by an electrician who hates the power company. Every room has a skylight, and every socket is treated as a threat.

Over the last decade the vocabulary has grown baroque. His posts on X now cite "Marcus inversion wells," the "Berry phase," "EZ water," cardiolipin geometry and a weakening of Earth's magnetic field that he ties to cancer, cavities and NFL injuries. His fans use three emojis as shorthand: a sun, a water drop, a magnet. He calls the mainstream "centralized medicine" and his own work "decentralized."

## Where he practised

The public paper trail is short and ordinary. In 2012 *The Tennessean* called him a Nashville neurosurgeon. In February 2013 the Louisiana medical board's minutes list "Kruse, John J." among physicians approved for reinstatement or relicensure, a routine step for a doctor returning to a state. In October 2017 he joined Our Lady of the Lake Physician Group in Slidell, Louisiana, across the lake from New Orleans.

His site later described him as CEO of the Kruse Longevity Center at Destin, Florida, "in private practice in the Gulf South." His LinkedIn now names a Kruse Longevity Center of El Salvador, and his posts praise the country as a place to live. In searches of board minutes and news coverage we found no published disciplinary action against him. As of autumn 2026, jackkruse.com shows a "coming soon" page; the old blog survives in the Internet Archive, and the new writing lives on Patreon.

## The voice on X

The Patreon costs five dollars a month at the entry tier, he tells followers. YouTube banned him around 2023, he says, so any channel there in his name is someone else. X is where he lives in public.

Spend an hour there and you learn the dialect. Posts end with "Few." or "IYKYK" (if you know, you know). A correct prediction earns "BOOM." Followers are "my tribe." Critics get worse: he told one critic writing about insulin resistance that he was "biochemical failure with a degree," and answered "cite the science, old fat man" with an obscenity and the year 2005. Between the biophysics he reposts forecasts of a banking collapse, a pole shift and vaccine "turbo cancer," plus conspiracy posts about financiers and Israel.

The part that should make you stop is the cancer replies. Grieving relatives tag him under posts about a sister's colon cancer or a mother's glioblastoma, and he answers with physics: the colonoscope's light, the magnetic field of her zip code, a Patreon link.

> He built a kingdom on the one thing nobody can bill you for: the sun.

## What to make of him

Be generous first. Kruse was talking about morning light, dark evenings and meal timing years before podcasters made them mainstream, and he has sat beside Andrew Huberman on Rick Rubin's *Tetragrammaton*. Several of his habits are cheap, pleasant and backed by decent science. The next four pages sort which ones.

Be honest second. The theory explains everything, so nothing can disprove it. The proof is mostly his own reading of other people's papers. And the tone is part of the product: certainty sells, and contempt keeps the tribe tight.

Someone once replied to him: why are you always so insufferable, when you could make your point with kindness? He answered with one word. "correct."

## Before you try anything

Kruse is a surgeon with a following, and a reply on X is not medical care. If you or someone you love has cancer, take his ideas to an oncologist as questions, never as a replacement for treatment. Cold water and big diet changes have real risks if you have heart disease, diabetes or take medication.

Strip the physics away and one claim sits in the middle of his whole system: that light hitting your eyes and skin at the right hour is food for your cells. On that one, oddly, the textbooks mostly agree with him. Next: what sunrise actually does to your body.
