Americans are getting colder. Stanford researchers compared temperature records from Union Army veterans of the Civil War with modern patients and found the average body has cooled by about 0.03 °C with every decade of birth since the 1800s. The famous 37 °C is a nineteenth-century number.

Ray Peat would have read that paper with a grim little smile. He spent his career saying modern bodies run cold, and he had a theory for why, a cure that sounds like dessert, and a strange fondness for a gas you've been taught to treat as exhaust. By the end of this page you'll know what that gas is and why he called it protective.

## The dial in your neck

Your thyroid is a butterfly-shaped gland under your Adam's apple. It makes two hormones: T4, the storage form, and T3, the active one. T3 tells cells how fast to burn fuel. Turn it up and you get heat, appetite, a quicker pulse and a sharper mind. Turn it down and you get cold hands, slow bowels, dry skin, low mood and rising cholesterol.

For Peat, this gland was **the master dial of the whole body**. Almost every complaint he wrote about, from insomnia to infertility, came back to cells burning too slowly.

He also believed medicine had lost the ability to see it. Before World War II, doctors diagnosed low thyroid by measuring a person's metabolic rate and looking at the whole patient. Then came blood tests: first a protein-bound iodine test in the late 1940s, later TSH, the pituitary's "make more thyroid" signal. In his telling, each new test shrank the number of people called hypothyroid, and the marketing of thyroid drugs did the rest. He called it fifty years of fraud.

He suspected a healthy TSH sits below 1, well under the usual lab cut-off of around 4. That's his opinion, and mainstream endocrinology disagrees with it. The cholesterol link, though, is textbook: low thyroid raises cholesterol, and thyroid treatment brings it down.

## Two numbers you can take at home

Peat's home test was borrowed from the physician Broda Barnes: a thermometer and a pulse.

He pointed to 37 °C (98.6 °F) as the healthy reference and noted that healthier groups average a resting pulse near 85 beats a minute, against around 70 in sicker ones. He told people to measure on waking and again around meals, because a warm room or a stress response can fake a good number. Adrenaline, after all, also heats you up.

Here the Stanford paper bites both ways. Peat would say the cooling proves his point. The authors suggest a kinder reason: fewer chronic infections, better living conditions, a body with less to fight. A thermometer can tell you that you're cool. It can't tell you why.

## Sugar as clean fuel

Now the dessert. Peat thought **sugar was the body's preferred, cleanest fuel**, and that fearing it made people sick.

His logic runs through stress. When blood sugar dips, your body releases adrenaline and cortisol to raise it again. Cortisol does this partly by breaking down muscle protein into glucose. Adrenaline releases stored fat, and those free fatty acids flood the blood and get in the way of burning glucose, a seesaw the biochemist Philip Randle described in 1963. In Peat's world, a sugar-starved body lives in a quiet, permanent emergency.

He compressed it into one line: "Unsaturated fatty acids activate the stress hormones, sugar restrains them."

So his sweet list: ripe fruit, orange juice, honey, milk, and even plain white sugar in a pinch. He preferred these to starch, argued that sucrose fattened rats less than starch did, and dismissed the glycemic index as meaningless. He liked fructose in particular, because it can enter energy pathways even when fat is blocking glucose. He also claimed the liver needs glucose to turn T4 into active T3.

That last claim has real support. In a 1976 study, obese volunteers on an 800-calorie diet with zero carbohydrate saw their T3 fall by 47%, about as much as during a total fast. The same people on equally low-calorie diets with at least 50 grams of carbohydrate a day kept their T3 steady. **Carbohydrate is a genuine signal for thyroid activation.** Score one for the orange juice.

## His case against low-carb

From there, Peat's verdict on low-carb and ketogenic diets was easy: they push the body into the stress state on purpose. Less T3, more cortisol, more fat in the blood, muscle used as backup sugar.

The evidence is messier than either camp likes. In a 2012 Boston feeding study, people who had just lost weight burned the most energy on a very-low-carb diet, the opposite of what Peat would predict. Their urine also showed the highest cortisol, which is exactly what he would predict. A lower T3 on low-carb may be a harmless adaptation in some people and a real drag in others. Nobody has run the trial that settles it.

And sugar has its own bill. When overweight adults drank fructose-sweetened drinks supplying a quarter of their daily calories for ten weeks, they gained belly fat and lost insulin sensitivity, while a glucose group didn't. Peat's sugar was meant for a lean, active body swapping starch and seed oil for fruit, inside its energy needs. Stacked on top of a surplus, it behaves very differently. Sweet talk only works on a body that isn't already full.

## The gas you were taught to get rid of

Here's the twist. Peat thought carbon dioxide, the stuff you breathe out, was one of the body's great protectors. He called it "a fundamental, universal protective factor."

Behind the idea sits a real textbook fact. In 1904 the Danish physiologist Christian Bohr showed that carbon dioxide and acidity loosen haemoglobin's grip on oxygen. Where tissue is working hard and making CO2, the blood lets go of more oxygen. This is the Bohr effect, and every medical student learns it.

Peat built outward from there. Over-breathing blows off CO2, so haemoglobin clings to its oxygen and blood vessels tighten; that's why panicky hyperventilation brings tingling and spasms. He argued that chronically low CO2 pushes cells toward lactic acid and inflammation, and he admired creatures that live in CO2-rich burrows, like the remarkably long-lived naked mole rat.

Then he closed the loop with sugar. Burning glucose produces about one molecule of CO2 for every molecule of oxygen used. Burning fat produces only about 0.7. So a body running on sugar, with a brisk thyroid, makes more CO2, which in his model delivers oxygen better, which keeps the furnace hot.

> In Peat's body, the fire makes its own draught.

It's elegant, and the pieces are real. The leap is in the conclusion. The Bohr effect is a local, moment-to-moment adjustment. Nobody has shown that raising your overall CO2 makes you healthier, and people whose lungs retain too much of it, as in severe lung disease, are not thriving.

## Before you try anything

Thyroid hormone is a prescription drug. Too much of it causes a racing heart, rhythm problems and bone loss, and self-treating based on a thermometer is how people get there. If you suspect a slow thyroid, ask a doctor for TSH and free T4, and bring your symptoms along. If you have diabetes or prediabetes, sugar is not a free fuel for you; work it out with your doctor.

So sugar opens the damper. What shuts it? Peat's answer sits in your kitchen cupboard, in a clear plastic bottle, and he called it toxic. The next page is about seed oils, and about two lost trials that surfaced decades later.
