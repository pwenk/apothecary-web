Somewhere tonight a twenty-four-year-old is grating a raw carrot into a bowl, pouring orange juice into a wine glass and counting his pulse with two fingers on his throat. He never met the man who told him to do any of it.

That man died in November 2022, at 86. By the end of this page you'll know who he was, why strangers still treat his essays like letters addressed to them personally, and the one habit of his that makes critics so furious they keep arguing with a dead biologist.

## A painter in a lab coat

Raymond Peat was born in 1936 and came to biology late, by way of the humanities and the easel. He taught art and painted portraits for a few years. Then he went back to school.

In 1972 the University of Oregon in Eugene gave him a PhD in biology, with a specialisation in physiology. He had started working on progesterone in 1968, published papers on it in 1971 and 1972, and wrote his dissertation around one idea: **progesterone and its relatives protect the body's structure and energy** against estrogen, radiation, stress and low oxygen.

He then taught at a scatter of places: the University of Oregon, Montana State University, the National College of Naturopathic Medicine, two Mexican universities and a small school called Blake College. He also gave private nutrition counselling. Eugene stayed home base for the rest of his life.

Notice what's missing from that CV. No big lab, no clinical trials, no hospital. Hold that thought; it comes back at the end.

## The one idea under everything

Peat had a single theory, and every opinion he held grows out of it like branches from a trunk.

Health, he said, is a high rate of energy production in every cell. A healthy cell burns its fuel completely, with oxygen, and turns out heat, carbon dioxide and work. A stressed cell burns dirty: it leans on stress hormones, spills lactic acid and runs cold.

Think of a wood stove. Dry logs and an open damper give you a hot, clean flame. Wet logs and a shut damper give you smoke, soot and a cold room. Peat spent fifty years listing what opens the damper and what shuts it.

His list of dampers-open: thyroid hormone, sugar from fruit and milk, saturated fat, progesterone, carbon dioxide, gelatin. His list of dampers-shut: polyunsaturated seed oils, estrogen, serotonin, chronic cortisol, endotoxin from an irritated gut. Each of those gets its own essay in this series.

He borrowed freely from older scientists who had fallen out of fashion. Otto Warburg, who argued in the 1920s that cancer cells have broken respiration. Broda Barnes, the physician who diagnosed low thyroid with a thermometer. Hans Selye, the father of the stress concept. Peat's followers later named the whole package **bioenergetics**, and the name stuck.

## The archive and the newsletter

Peat built no company and ran no clinic chain. What he left is text.

There's raypeat.com, a plain website with dozens of long essays: thyroid, fats, sugar, estrogen, serotonin, aging, cancer. They read like a seminar transcript. A paragraph of argument, then a stack of citations, many from old Russian and mid-century journals nobody else was reading. He had a gift for mockery dressed as patience. In one thyroid essay he sneers at "the doctrine of the impenetrable membrane barrier," the textbook idea that hormones need special doors to enter cells.

Then there was the newsletter, which subscribers could still get printed and posted, for a small fee, long after the rest of the world had moved to screens. And a handful of books: *Mind and Tissue* (1976), *Nutrition for Women* (1981), *Generative Energy* (1994), *From PMS to Menopause* (1997).

He also gave hours of radio and podcast interviews, slow and digressive, where callers described their symptoms and he answered with enzyme names. People wrote to him directly, and he often wrote back. That intimacy is half the cult. A man with a PhD answered your email about your cold hands.

## The Peaters

For decades Peat was a niche read for naturopaths and women's-health searchers. Then, in the 2010s, forums, podcasts and later Twitter and TikTok found him.

The fans call themselves Peaters, and the diet acquired a marketing name, "pro-metabolic." Online it shrank to a shopping list: milk, orange juice, gelatin gummies, ice cream, coffee with sugar, the raw carrot salad, and absolutely no seed oil. The carrot salad even had a viral moment. Search his name and you'll find him called a quack and a genius in the same page of results.

The shopping list is a bit unfair to him. Peat wrote in paragraphs full of "in some conditions" and "for some people." The internet kept the nouns and dropped the conditions. Still, he gave it plenty to work with, and he never seemed bothered to see his ideas travel without their footnotes.

There's also a rivalry. Another camp of energy-and-mitochondria enthusiasts, gathered around a neurosurgeon named Jack Kruse, thinks the Peaters are drinking the wrong juice entirely. We'll get there.

## Why people still argue about him

Two things keep the fight alive.

First, he was sometimes early and right enough to sting. He spent decades attacking seed oils and the low-fat orthodoxy; then, in 2013 and 2016, researchers dug up lost data from two old vegetable-oil trials that looked bad for the oils. He mocked serotonin as a "happiness" molecule; in 2022 a large review found no solid evidence that low serotonin causes depression. When a contrarian lands a few punches like that, his fans assume he was right about everything else too.

Second, his evidence has a pattern. It's built from animal experiments, test-tube studies, old clinical reports and his own reasoning, stitched into a story. He almost never pointed to a trial in people that tested his actual advice, because almost none exist. And he rarely said what result would change his mind.

> Read Peat for his questions, then go and check every answer he gave.

That's the habit that drives critics up the wall: a mechanism in a rat liver becomes a breakfast recommendation for you. His defenders reply that mainstream nutrition did the same thing with cholesterol for fifty years. Both sides have a point, and the next four essays put real numbers on it.

## Before you try anything

Peat's world isn't only food. It includes thyroid hormone, progesterone and aspirin, real drugs with real side effects and interactions. If you have thyroid disease, diabetes, a heart or kidney condition, are pregnant, or take blood thinners, talk to a doctor and get bloodwork before borrowing anything from him.

So start where he started: the body's thermostat. Peat thought most modern people run about a degree too cold, and that a glass of orange juice and a working thyroid could warm them back up. The next page takes his thermometer seriously, and asks why sugar, of all things, was his favourite fuel.
