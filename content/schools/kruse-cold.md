One number launched a million ice baths: 250 percent. It's the rise in dopamine that podcasters promise you from cold water, and it gets quoted like a law of nature.

It comes from one Czech study of a small group of young men in the year 2000. By the end of this page you'll know what it measured, and why it isn't the dopamine people think it is. First, the man who made ice a lifestyle a decade before it had its own aisle in the shop.

## Kruse's freezer gospel

In 2012 Jack Kruse started blogging about cold thermogenesis, and his site later carried a "Cold Thermogenesis Easy Start Guide." CT, he wrote, belongs alongside his leptin reset and works best at sunrise or after dinner. You begin by dunking your face in a sink of ice water for as long as you can stand it. Then you lie down with a block of ice on your chest over a compression shirt. Then you get into a bathtub with ice in it.

The promised benefits ran to a long list: lower body fat, higher hormones, fertility, reversed diabetes, a fixed thyroid, deep sleep, pain relief, and "kill fat permanently." If cold made your skin break out in hives, he wrote, that meant your tissues held too much omega-6 fat. No study supports that reading of hives.

His reasoning was evolutionary. Mammals adapted through ice ages, so cold should switch on ancient fat-burning machinery and make mitochondria lean and efficient, especially paired with DHA from cold-water seafood. He still preaches it: in 2026 he told a stranger on X with nerve pain to soak his feet in cold water and eat more DHA.

Some of that machinery is real. In 2012, when he started, most doctors had only just learned adults had it at all.

## The fat that burns

Most of your fat is white fat, a pantry. **Brown fat** is a furnace. Its cells are packed with mitochondria that carry a protein called **UCP1**, uncoupling protein 1.

Normally a mitochondrion pumps protons across its inner membrane and lets them flow back through a turbine that makes ATP. UCP1 opens a side door. The protons rush back without turning the turbine, and the energy leaves as heat. It's a car revving in neutral to warm the cabin.

Babies have plenty of brown fat. Adults were assumed to have lost it until 2009, when three papers in the *New England Journal of Medicine* showed otherwise. Aaron Cypess's team, reading PET scans from nearly two thousand patients, found active brown fat more often in women, in leaner people and in the colder months.

Then came the training studies from Maastricht. In 2013 Anouk van der Lans and colleagues kept volunteers in a mildly cold room for hours a day over ten days. Their brown fat grew more active, they burned more energy without shivering, and they reported feeling less cold. In 2015 Mark Hanssen's team ran the same idea in eight men with type 2 diabetes. After ten days, their insulin sensitivity rose by about 43 percent, mostly through muscle taking up more sugar rather than through brown fat.

**Cold genuinely remodels metabolism, and it can do it in days.** The catch: the extra calories burned are modest, and appetite tends to rise to meet them.

## Søberg's winter swimmers

Copenhagen's winter swimmers alternate ice-water dips with hot saunas. In 2021 Susanna Søberg and colleagues studied young men who did this two or three times a week and compared them with non-swimmers.

The swimmers ran slightly cooler at rest, and their brown fat sat idle in comfortable conditions. Once cooled, they ramped heat production up faster than the controls. The authors read it as both heat and cold acclimation, the body learning to let its temperature swing.

The internet boiled this into a weekly cold-water quota. The paper measured habits in a small group of Danes; it never tested a dose.

## The 250 percent

Back to the number. In 2000 Petr Šrámek and colleagues at Charles University in Prague immersed young men up to the neck for an hour in water at 32, 20 and 14 °C. The 14 °C hour was brutal: metabolic rate up 350 percent, plasma noradrenaline up 530 percent, plasma dopamine up 250 percent. Cortisol, the stress hormone everyone expects to spike, tended to fall.

Here's the twist the podcasts skip. **That dopamine was measured in blood, and blood dopamine is not brain dopamine.** Dopamine doesn't cross from the blood into the brain. What circulates comes largely from nerves, the adrenal glands and the gut, and it does jobs like regulating blood vessels and the kidneys. The [dopamine](/apothecary/dopamine) of motivation and desire lives behind the blood-brain barrier, and this study didn't look there.

The surge of alertness after a cold plunge is real; noradrenaline alone would do it. Calling it a 250 percent dopamine boost is a borrowed number on the wrong organ.

> Cold is a bill your body pays in noradrenaline, and the receipt reads like euphoria.

## The bill

Most people who die in cold water die in the first minutes, from cold shock: an involuntary gasp, runaway breathing, a heart rate and blood pressure spike, and in some hearts a dangerous rhythm. Mike Tipton's 2017 review in *Experimental Physiology* walks through it and finds the evidence for most claimed benefits still weak.

There's a cost for lifters too. In 2015 Llion Roberts's group had men take cold baths after strength sessions for twelve weeks. They gained less muscle and strength than men who cooled down actively instead. Cold blunts the signals that tell muscle to grow.

## What holds up, what doesn't

- **Holds:** adults have brown fat; repeated cold makes it more active and raises energy burn; short cold acclimation improved insulin sensitivity in a small diabetes trial.
- **Holds:** cold water sharply raises noradrenaline and alertness.
- **Misread:** "250 percent dopamine" is blood, not brain.
- **Overstated:** "kill fat permanently," fixed thyroids, reversed diabetes from ice baths alone; the calorie effect is modest.
- **Unsupported:** cold hives as a readout of omega-6 in tissue.

## Before you try anything

Cold shock in the first minute is the real danger. Heart disease, high blood pressure, Raynaud's, pregnancy, and medicines that affect heart rhythm all deserve a doctor's view first. Never get into cold water alone, never hyperventilate before it, and if you lift for size, know that ice right after training works against you.

Kruse's cold was always a means. He believed it made mitochondria burn cleaner and freed the body of something heavy it doesn't need: a double-weight hydrogen hiding in every glass of water you drink. Next: deuterium, and the breakfast he said could rewire your brain.
