You have a kind of eye cell that doesn't help you see anything. It can't make out a face or a word. It only asks one question, all day long: how bright is it, and how blue?

Scientists pinned it down in 2002. It is the main reason Jack Kruse wants you outdoors before breakfast. By the end of this page you'll know which of his four big light claims are textbook, which are promising, and which one would make a physicist reach for the red pen.

## The eye that doesn't see

Most of your retina is rods and cones, the cells that make pictures. Scattered among the nerve cells behind them sits a small population that carries its own light-catching pigment, **melanopsin**. In 2002 Samer Hattar's team showed these cells sense light directly and run a cable straight to the suprachiasmatic nucleus, or SCN: about twenty thousand neurons above the crossing of your optic nerves that serve as your master clock.

Melanopsin is most sensitive to blue light, roughly the blue of a clear morning sky. Bright light in the morning tells the SCN "day starts now," and every organ's clock sets itself from that.

The clock drifts if you don't remind it. Think of a piano that slips a little out of tune every night and gets retuned each morning by daylight. Indoors, the tuner barely shows up: an office is often dozens of times dimmer than daylight outdoors.

Kenneth Wright's group tested this in 2013. They sent eight people camping in the Colorado mountains for a week with no electric light, only sun and campfire. Their internal clocks moved about two hours earlier and lined up with sunrise and sunset. **Kruse's oldest rule, see the morning sun, sits on solid ground.** His version is stricter (sunrise itself, eyes unshaded, no glass in between) and those details are his, but the core is mainstream sleep science.

## The melatonin thief

Melatonin is the hormone your brain releases at dusk to say "night." Light shuts it off.

In 2011 Joshua Gooley and colleagues at Harvard compared 116 young adults living in ordinary room light in the evening with people kept in dim light. Room light delayed the start of melatonin in 99 percent of them and shortened its nightly run by about 90 minutes. That was plain indoor lighting, before anyone counted phones.

Kruse goes further. He calls blue light at night a toxin and blames it for diabetes, cancer and bone loss. On X he once linked blue-lit gyms to osteoporosis and weak muscles through "the spin state of oxygen." The melatonin part holds. The jump from a later melatonin onset to brittle bones is his.

## Ultraviolet: vitamin D, a tan and a little opiate

Everyone knows ultraviolet B (UVB) makes vitamin D in skin. Kruse's point is that vitamin D is only one item on the bill, and here the science has caught up with some of his enthusiasm.

In 2007 David Fisher's lab at Harvard showed what happens when UV hits a skin cell. Damage switches on p53, the cell's guardian protein, which turns on a gene called POMC. The cell cuts that protein into pieces. One piece is **alpha-MSH**, the hormone that tells pigment cells to tan you. Another is **beta-endorphin**, a natural opiate. The authors suggested it may help explain why people seek the sun. MSH is the same family of signals behind the tanning-and-libido peptides on the [melanocortins](/apothecary/melanocortins) page.

Then there's nitric oxide, the gas that relaxes blood vessels. In 2014 Richard Weller and Martin Feelisch's team shone UVA on the skin of 24 healthy volunteers. Their blood pressure dropped, because UVA released nitric oxide that was already stored in the skin.

Kruse's conclusion is that sunscreen and sun avoidance are disasters. The science says UV is a real drug with real benefits and a real cost: sunburn and skin cancer. Both ledgers are true.

## Red light and the enzyme that drinks it

Deep red and near-infrared light pass through skin into tissue. The last enzyme in the mitochondrial power chain, **cytochrome c oxidase**, absorbs those wavelengths. The leading theory of "photobiomodulation" says that absorption helps mitochondria make more ATP, the cell's energy currency.

The best-known human work comes from Glen Jeffery at University College London. In 2020 his team gave people aged 28 to 72 brief daily exposures to 670-nanometre deep red light for two weeks. People under 40 showed nothing. People around 40 and older saw better on a colour-contrast test, in the blue range where ageing retinas fail first. His group has since published small studies on red light and blood sugar.

Kruse takes this much further. Sunlight's infrared, he says, structures the water in your cells (borrowing Gerald Pollack's "EZ water"), and your mitochondria are tuned quantum devices. In 2026 a paper reporting a 71-terahertz vibration in mitochondria went viral among his followers. **Red light on mitochondria has some evidence; the quantum-water story is a hypothesis wearing a lab coat.**

## The nnEMF fear

Kruse's own coinage, nnEMF, means non-native electromagnetic fields: Wi-Fi, phones, LEDs, power lines, the magnetic hum of old fluorescent ballasts. He advises living on the ground floor, grounding, and keeping devices out of the bedroom.

Some of this has a paper trail. The WHO's cancer agency classes radio-frequency fields (2011) and low-frequency magnetic fields (2002) as "possibly carcinogenic," group 2B, the same shelf as aloe vera leaf extract. The magnetic-field listing rests on an odd, unexplained link between unusually high home exposures and childhood leukaemia.

The biggest test went the other way. In 2024 a review commissioned by the WHO, led by Ken Karipidis, pooled decades of studies and found no link between mobile phone use and brain cancers. Evidence that Wi-Fi drives everyday disease is thin.

> Morning light is the cheapest drug you'll ever be prescribed, and the only one that comes up by itself.

## What holds up, what doesn't

- **Holds:** morning daylight anchors the clock; bright light in the evening delays and shrinks melatonin.
- **Partly holds:** UV does more than vitamin D (MSH, endorphins, nitric oxide), with skin cancer as the price.
- **Some evidence:** red and near-infrared light on mitochondria, from small trials.
- **Thin:** nnEMF as a major cause of disease; structured water and quantum mitochondria as the explanation for it all.

## Before you try anything

Never look straight at the sun, at sunrise or any other time. If you've had skin cancer, are fair-skinned, or take drugs that make skin sensitive to light (some antibiotics such as [doxycycline](/apothecary/doxycycline), some acne and heart medicines), talk to a doctor before you chase UV. People with bipolar disorder should get advice before bright-light routines, since light can tip mood.

Kruse's second lever doesn't come from the sky. It comes out of the freezer, it started with a man's face in a sink, and it gave the internet a dopamine number that almost everyone quotes wrong. Next: cold.
