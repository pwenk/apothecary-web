Ray Peat said a raw carrot cured his migraines. One a day, eaten plain, and the headaches stopped.

By the end of this page you'll know what he believed the carrot was carrying out of his body, why that belief set him against nearly every gynaecologist alive, and the one place where a big French study quietly took his side.

## The alarm system

Peat's hormone theory is his energy theory wearing a lab coat. When a cell can't make enough energy, the body sounds alarms to cope. In his model those alarms are cortisol, estrogen and serotonin, backed up by free fatty acids and lactic acid. The protectors, the hormones that let the alarms fall quiet, are progesterone, pregnenolone and thyroid hormone.

Mainstream biology agrees with him about one of the three. Cortisol really is the body's main stress hormone. Calling estrogen and serotonin stress signals is where **he walks off the textbook page**, and this essay flags each step.

## Estrogen, the hormone he distrusted most

Peat argued that estrogen, sold for decades as protection for women's bones, brains and hearts, actually drives degenerative aging. He cited animal and lab studies showing estrogen activates the stress axis, raising the brain signal ACTH and then cortisol. He claimed it overexcites nerve cells, interferes with energy production in mitochondria and, with seed oils, feeds a vicious circle of inflammation.

His counterweight was progesterone. "Progesterone is the basic brain-protective antiestrogen," he wrote.

Here's the departure, plainly. Mainstream medicine treats estrogen as essential to bone, blood vessels, skin and brain. The North American Menopause Society's 2022 position statement calls hormone therapy the most effective treatment for hot flushes and says it prevents bone loss and fractures, with benefits usually outweighing risks for healthy women under 60 or within ten years of menopause. **On estrogen as a villain, Peat stands almost alone.**

## Progesterone and pregnenolone, the bodyguards

Peat started his scientific life with progesterone, and he never stopped loving it. He described it as the body's most protective hormone, the basic hormone of adaptation to stress: calming to nerves, protective of the brain, an anti-estrogen everywhere.

Pregnenolone was its quiet parent. It's the raw material the body makes progesterone, DHEA and other steroids from. Peat told its story as an injustice: identified in 1934, written off as inert because researchers tested it on healthy young animals who already had plenty, then studied in the 1940s for fatigue and performance in stressed people, then abandoned, he claimed, because a natural molecule couldn't be patented.

Two pieces of human data are worth knowing.

The first is on his side. In France, the E3N study followed 80,377 postmenopausal women. Those using estrogen with real, micronised progesterone had no rise in breast cancer risk (relative risk 1.00), while estrogen combined with synthetic progestins raised it by 69%. Peat had spent years insisting that synthetic progestins are not progesterone. **That distinction has held up.**

The second isn't. In ProTECT III, published in 2014, 882 people with acute traumatic brain injury got progesterone or placebo within hours. The trial was stopped early: progesterone did no better, and caused more vein inflammation. The brain protection that looked so strong in rodents didn't show up in a human emergency.

## Serotonin, the hibernation signal

The popular story calls serotonin the happiness molecule. Peat thought that was public relations.

In his model serotonin is an inhibitory, defensive signal that slows energy production, like a hibernating animal pulling down the shutters. He linked it to inflammation, to raised cortisol and to the body's response to a stressed gut. That last link has a real anchor: most of the body's serotonin is made in the intestine, not the brain. In Peat's reading, an irritated gut leaking bacterial toxins pumps out serotonin, and the whole body feels it.

Some modern findings rhyme with him. In 2022 an umbrella review led by the psychiatrist Joanna Moncrieff found no consistent evidence that depression is caused by low serotonin. In mice, blocking serotonin production outside the brain protected the animals from obesity on a high-fat diet by turning up their heat-burning brown fat.

But the leap from "low serotonin doesn't explain depression" to "serotonin is the enemy" is his alone. A 2018 analysis of 522 trials found all 21 antidepressants tested beat placebo, many of them drugs that boost serotonin signalling. **Serotonin as villain is a contrarian position**, interesting and mostly untested.

## Cortisol, the one everyone agrees on

Cortisol frees sugar and fat for fuel and breaks down protein to make more. In an emergency, that's lifesaving; people whose adrenals can't make it can die from an ordinary infection. Chronic excess is a different animal: thin skin, wasted muscle, weak bones and high blood sugar, the picture doctors call Cushing's syndrome.

Peat's twist was causal. He saw cortisol rising because energy is short, and thought sugar, thyroid and progesterone lower the body's need for it. That's a hypothesis, and parts of it fit what the last essay showed about carbohydrate and T3.

> In Peat's body, the hormones you were told to want are the ones that sound the alarm.

## The toolkit

Peat's world came with tools, each chosen to quiet one of the alarms.

**Aspirin.** He thought of it as a near-universal protector, with effects "approximately opposite" to estrogen's: anti-inflammatory, protective of the brain, hostile to tumours. Mainstream medicine has narrowed aspirin's role. In the ASPREE trial, healthy older adults taking daily aspirin gained no benefit and bled more often, and slightly more of them died, mostly of cancer. The scorecard returns to this.

**Gelatin.** Muscle meat is rich in tryptophan, the raw material for serotonin, and in cysteine, both of which Peat believed slowed the thyroid. Gelatin, cooked collagen, is mostly glycine, a calming amino acid. He liked it as a counterweight to steak.

**The raw carrot.** Back to the migraines. Peat reported that several women with premenopausal symptoms, migraine among them, had their blood estrogen measured before and after a daily carrot, and that it fell within days. His explanation: carrot fibre speeds things through the gut, so estrogen the liver has dumped into bile gets carried out before it can be reabsorbed. Elsewhere he recommended the carrot grated, with salt and vinegar, to cut absorption of bacterial toxins. The only salad with a hormone agenda.

Is there anything to it? A 1991 study gave 62 premenopausal women extra wheat bran, doubling their fibre to about 30 grams a day. After two months their estrogen levels had fallen. Oat and corn bran did nothing. So **fibre can lower circulating estrogen**, depending on the fibre. The carrot itself has never been tested.

## Before you try anything

Progesterone, pregnenolone, DHEA and aspirin are drugs, even when sold over the counter. Hormones can disturb cycles, fertility and clotting; aspirin causes bleeding and interacts with blood thinners. Never stop prescribed hormone therapy or antidepressants on the strength of an essay. Ask a doctor, and get your levels measured before anyone adjusts them.

Peat has now told you what to eat, what to fear and which hormones to trust. Time to grade him, claim by claim, including a bedtime habit of his that holds up better than you'd guess. The last essay is the scorecard.
