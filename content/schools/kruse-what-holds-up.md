Jack Kruse has one habit worth stealing. It costs nothing, takes a few minutes, and works even if every word of his physics turns out to be wrong.

By the end of this page you'll know which habit, why it works, and the three places where Kruse and Ray Peat, two men whose fans can't stand each other, would have shaken hands. First the bill, line by line.

## The scorecard

| Kruse's claim | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Morning daylight sets your body clock | Holds | Melanopsin cells wire light to the brain's master clock; a week of camping moved people's clocks about two hours earlier |
| Bright light at night steals melatonin | Holds | Ordinary room light delayed melatonin in 99% of young adults and cut its nightly run by about 90 minutes |
| The sun does far more than vitamin D | Partly | UV makes MSH and beta-endorphin and lowers blood pressure through nitric oxide; skin cancer is the cost |
| Red and infrared light feed mitochondria | Promising, small | Deep red light improved ageing vision in a small trial; mechanism still debated |
| Cold builds brown fat and fixes metabolism | Partly | Ten days of mild cold raised brown fat activity, and insulin sensitivity in eight men with diabetes; calorie effect modest |
| Cold water floods the brain with dopamine | Misread | The famous 250% was measured in blood, which doesn't reach the brain |
| A big protein breakfast tames hunger | Partly | Less hunger in equal-calorie trials, but no extra weight loss |
| The leptin reset rewires the hypothalamus | Untested | No trial of the protocol itself |
| Deuterium-depleted water treats disease | Thin | Cell studies and small series, mostly from one research circle |
| Phones and Wi-Fi are a major cause of disease | Not supported | A WHO-commissioned review found no link between mobile phones and brain cancer |
| Earth's weakening magnetic field explains cancer, cavities and sports injuries | Unsupported | No data offered beyond his own reasoning |

Read down the column and a pattern shows. **The closer a claim stays to sunrise, darkness and meal timing, the better it does.** The further it travels into quantum water and magnetic zip codes, the less there is underneath.

## Where he's earned his credit

He was early. In 2011, when diet forums argued about grams of carbohydrate, Kruse was telling people that when they ate and what light they saw mattered as much as what was on the plate. A decade later that became mainstream circadian science and a staple of the biggest health podcasts.

His habits are mostly cheap and pleasant: daylight, dark evenings, seafood, walking outside, fewer snacks. Many gurus sell a product that's expensive and harmless. Kruse's best ideas are free and mildly helpful, which is a better ratio.

## Where the physics runs away

A theory that explains everything can't be caught being wrong. Kruse's model now accounts for obesity, cancer, tooth decay, NFL injuries and ocean currents, through light, water, magnetism and deuterium. When a prediction misses, the zip code's magnetic field or a hidden nnEMF source takes the blame.

The vocabulary works like a moat. "Marcus inversion wells" and "the Berry phase" are real terms from physics, borrowed far from where they were defined. Followers who can't follow the words assume the fault is theirs, and he encourages it: stop complaining that the thesis is hard, he told them, and swallow it at your own pace.

The cost lands on the most frightened readers. A person with cancer who reads that a colonoscope's light helped cause a sister's tumour, or that a friend's cancer comes from her zip code's magnetic field, may choose treatment late or not at all. That's where generosity has to stop.

> Take his sunrise and leave his cosmology; the first one never needed the second.

## Kruse and Peat at the same table

You met [Ray Peat](/schools/ray-peat) earlier in this wing. The two schools fight online like rival football clubs, yet they share more than either side admits.

**Where they agree.** Both treat light as nourishment and darkness as a stressor. Both read metabolism as heat: Peat asked people to check their temperature and pulse, Kruse calls obesity a thermodynamic problem of energy lost to the environment. Both watch the thyroid closely, and both distrust industrial seed oils.

**Where they split.** Sugar first. Peat told people to drink orange juice and milk and to eat fruit, sugar as fuel for a hot metabolism. Kruse's leptin plan starts with a breakfast nearly free of carbs and treats sugar with suspicion; on X he insists glucose and fructose behave differently in the body for reasons mainstream nutrition misses. Then cold: Kruse sees it as medicine, while Peat saw the adrenaline rush of cold as stress to be kept low. Then fish: Kruse prizes DHA from seafood (his 2011 leptin plan even suggested fish oil), and Peat wrote an essay called "The great fish oil experiment" arguing those fragile fats do harm.

Picture two cooks at one stove. They agree the flame matters more than the recipe. One feeds it sugar, the other feeds it sunlight and seafood. Neither has run the trial that would settle it, and the full patterns of both remain untested against each other. [Peat's own scorecard](/schools/peat-what-holds-up) sits beside this one.

## Borrowing the sunrise without the cosmology

Here's the habit. Get real daylight on your face early in the day, outdoors, where it's many times brighter than any room. Let your evenings get dim and warm. Keep meal and sleep times steady, so your clocks have something to set themselves by.

It works because of plain biology from the light page: melanopsin, the SCN, melatonin. None of it needs deuterium, magnetic fields or quantum water. You don't need to believe the planet's magnetic field is collapsing to enjoy a coffee on the balcony at seven.

Notice what the body tells you after two weeks. Sleep onset, morning hunger, afternoon energy: those are the readouts that count. Let the body grade the habit.

## Before you try anything

Don't look at the sun directly. If you're fair, have had skin cancer, or take medicines that make skin sensitive to light, ask a doctor how much UV makes sense. Cold water deserves medical advice if you have heart or blood pressure problems. And anything sold to you as a cancer treatment, deuterium water included, goes past your oncologist first.

One footnote from the sun before you go. A tan comes from MSH, the same family of signals that also stirs desire. Yet when researchers in Scotland asked people which skin glow looked healthiest, the winner owed more to vegetables than to sunlight. Why some faces pull eyes across a room is older than any guru.
