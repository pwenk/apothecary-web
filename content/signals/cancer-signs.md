Cancer rarely opens with pain. The US National Cancer Institute says it plainly: don't wait to hurt before you see a doctor. What cancer does instead is whisper, and the whisper is easy to ignore because the same sound usually means something dull.

By the end of this page you'll know the three words that separate a dull whisper from one worth a visit, the nerve that lets a chest tumour steal your voice, and the famous screening test that has never managed to prove it saves lives.

Breathe first. Nearly every sign below is far more often caused by something harmless, and the American Cancer Society says so outright. The goal here is the state of a good night driver: relaxed hands, eyes open.

## How a cell goes rogue, and why it shows

Every cell in you carries brakes, genes that say "stop dividing", plus a self-destruct switch for when copying goes wrong. A cancer is a family of cells that has lost enough of those brakes to keep copying anyway.

The warning signs come from three things such cells do. They take up room and press on their neighbours. They bleed, because the blood vessels they grow for themselves are flimsy. And they provoke the immune system, which answers with chemical signals that change your appetite, your temperature and your energy.

Room, blood, signals. Almost every sign on the list belongs to one of those three, so let's take them in order.

## Things that take up room

**A new lump** in a breast, armpit, testicle, neck or groin. Most are cysts, fatty lumps, or lymph nodes swollen from an infection that shrink back within a few weeks. A lump that stays, grows, or feels hard and stuck in place has earned a pair of trained hands.

**Trouble swallowing**, the feeling that food catches on the way down. When a growth narrows the gullet, solids stick first and liquids only later. Reflux and muscle problems are the usual culprits, and doctors still like to look promptly, usually with a small camera.

**A change in bowel habits** that lasts: looser, more frequent, thinner, or the nagging sense you haven't quite finished. A growth in the bowel can narrow or irritate it. Food, stress and travel do the same thing for a week. The difference is the word "lasts".

**A cough or a hoarse voice for more than three weeks.** The NHS draws its line there. And here is the odd fact from the top. The nerve that works your left vocal cord takes an absurd detour: it drops down into the chest, hooks under the aorta, and climbs back up to the throat. A growth in the chest can squeeze it on the way past, so the first sign of a lung problem can be a voice that turns husky.

Pressure is one channel. The second is blood, and blood is the sign people most often talk themselves out of.

## Blood where it shouldn't be

Tumours grow their own blood supply, and those new vessels are thin-walled and leaky. So blood turns up in places it has no business being: in stool, or as black, tarry motions; in urine; in what you cough up; from the vagina between periods, after sex or after menopause; from a mole or a sore.

Haemorrhoids cause most rectal bleeding, and infections cause most blood in urine. Blood is still worth reporting every time, because it's one of the few signs that is easy to describe and quick to check.

Sometimes the bleeding is invisible. A slow leak from the bowel can drain iron for months, and all you notice is tiredness and breathlessness on the stairs. That leads to the third channel.

## The signals that change how you feel

The immune system fights a tumour with the same chemical messengers it uses against flu, called cytokines. They tell the brain to turn up the thermostat, cut appetite and save energy. For a week of flu, that's sensible. Kept up for months, it produces the slow signs.

**Weight loss you didn't try for.** The American Cancer Society puts the flag at about 10 pounds (roughly 4.5 kg) for no known reason. In a 2018 Oxford review of 25 studies, Brian Nicholson and colleagues found that weight loss a GP thought worth noting was linked to ten different cancers, and in patients over 60 the risk crossed the 3 percent line UK guidance uses to order tests. Read that number calmly: 97 in 100 of those people had something else.

**Night sweats** that soak the sheets, sometimes with fevers that come and go. Menopause, infections, anxiety and many common medicines cause most of them. Drenching sweats together with weight loss and fever form a pattern doctors look for in lymphoma, a cancer of the immune cells themselves.

**Tiredness that rest doesn't fix.** The ACS phrase is extreme tiredness not relieved by rest. Usually the cause is sleep, stress, low iron, or a thyroid running slow.

The last channel is the one organ you can inspect yourself, in a mirror, tonight.

## Your skin, read like a map

For moles, doctors use ABCDE. **A**symmetry: one half doesn't match the other. **B**order: ragged, notched or blurred. **C**olour: several shades inside one spot. **D**iameter: wider than 6 mm, about a pencil eraser, though melanomas can be smaller. **E**volving: changing in size, shape or colour. The ACS adds the ugly duckling sign, a spot that looks unlike all your others.

E carries the most weight. A mole that has looked odd for 20 years and never changed is less interesting than a plain one that started changing last spring.

Then there's **the sore that won't heal**. On the skin it might be a pearly bump or a scaly patch that crusts, bleeds and comes back. In the mouth it's an ulcer, or a white or red patch, that lingers. Skin closes a scratch in days. A spot that keeps reopening for a month deserves an audience.

Which brings us to the three words.

## New, persistent, unexplained

A sign deserves a doctor when it is **new** (different from your own normal), **persistent** (still there after about three weeks, or getting worse) and **unexplained** (no cold, no injury, no diet, no obvious reason).

Each word filters out a crowd. The mole you've had since school fails "new". A cough during a cold fails "unexplained" and usually "persistent" too. Weight lost on purpose fails "unexplained". What passes all three filters is uncommon, and it is exactly what the NHS, ACS and NCI describe in their own words: not normal for you, doesn't go away, no known cause.

> The body rarely shouts about cancer. It repeats itself, quietly, for weeks.

## Screening that earns its place

Symptoms are the body raising its hand. Screening looks for trouble before the hand goes up. Only a few tests have shown they save lives, and NCI lists them:

- **Colon:** stool tests or colonoscopy, ages 45 to 75 in the US. A colonoscopy can remove polyps before they ever turn into cancer.
- **Cervix:** HPV and Pap tests, ages 21 to 65. They catch changed cells years ahead and so prevent the disease outright.
- **Breast:** mammograms from 40 to 74; the US Preventive Services Task Force advised every two years in 2024.
- **Lung, for heavy smokers:** a yearly low-dose CT scan from 50 to 80 for people with at least 20 pack-years (a pack a day for 20 years) who still smoke or quit within the last 15. In the 2011 National Lung Screening Trial of more than 53,000 people, it cut lung cancer deaths by 20 percent compared with chest X-rays.

Ages vary by country, and many European programmes start some tests later, so ask your own doctor for the local schedule. Men between 55 and 69 can also discuss a PSA blood test for prostate cancer, a personal choice because it finds many slow cancers that would never have caused harm.

Now the surprise from the top: **skin checks**. A full-body exam by a doctor feels like the most obvious screening there is, yet NCI says such exams haven't been shown to lower the risk of dying from skin cancer, and the US task force called the evidence insufficient in 2023. What clearly helps is knowing your own skin and reporting a change promptly. People with many moles, a family history or a past skin cancer are a separate case and do benefit from regular checks.

## When to see someone

Book an ordinary appointment for anything that passes the three-word test. Ask for a quicker one for unexplained blood, trouble swallowing or a lump that is growing. Coughing up blood or vomiting blood calls for an urgent appointment that same day.

Bring a short note on your phone: what changed, when it started, what makes it better or worse, and any weight change in kilos. Doctors think in timelines, and you'll forget half of it once you're in the chair.

Expect dull first steps: an examination, blood tests, perhaps a scan or a camera. For most people they end in a harmless explanation and a lighter walk home, which is the whole reason to go.

One sign on the list, the tiredness sleep won't fix, has a far more common suspect, and it sits at the front of your neck: a gland shaped like a bow tie that sets the pace of every cell you own.
