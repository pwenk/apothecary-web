Most men notice it in the morning first. For years the body woke up before they did, and then, somewhere along the way, it started sleeping in.

Then the rest arrives slowly: less interest in sex, a flatter mood, a belly that ignores the gym, a bench press that stalls. It's easy to file all of it under "getting older" or "a hard year".

By the end of this page you'll know which of those signs actually point at testosterone, why the test has to be taken before breakfast and then taken again, and why the most popular fix can leave a man with almost no sperm at all.

## What testosterone does all day

Testosterone is made mostly in the testes, in Leydig cells that sit between the tubes where sperm are made. It is the hormone of drive in the broad sense: it feeds desire, builds muscle and bone, keeps red blood cells topped up, and nudges mood and energy upward. (The full molecule gets its own page at /apothecary/testosterone.)

The factory takes orders from the brain. The hypothalamus sends a pulse signal to the pituitary, and the pituitary releases two messengers. **LH** (luteinising hormone) tells the Leydig cells to make testosterone. **FSH** (follicle-stimulating hormone) tells the tubes to make sperm. Hold onto those two letters, because they come back twice.

Levels follow a daily tide. They're highest in the early morning and lowest by evening, and a sugary meal pulls them down for a few hours. Both facts matter enormously when you test.

## Which signs actually count

The Endocrine Society's 2018 guideline, led by Shalender Bhasin, splits the signs into two piles.

The more specific pile: **lower sexual desire, fewer spontaneous and morning erections**, breast tenderness or swelling, loss of body hair, smaller or softer testes, low sperm count, and hot flushes.

The vaguer pile: low energy, low mood, poor concentration, sleepiness, smaller muscles and less strength, and more body fat, especially around the waist.

The first pile is your clue. The second pile alone describes half the men in any office, and most of them have normal testosterone and too little sleep. A **fading morning erection together with fading desire** is the pairing worth a blood test.

So, what does the test look like?

## Before breakfast, and then again

The guideline asks for a **fasting, early-morning** blood sample, because that's when the tide is highest and food hasn't muddied it. Testosterone drawn at 4 p.m. after lunch can look low in a perfectly healthy man.

Then it asks for **a second test** on another morning. Single results bounce around: a bad night, a cold, a heavy weekend. Only symptoms plus two clearly low results add up to a diagnosis, which doctors call hypogonadism (testes that underproduce). Sometimes they also measure SHBG, the protein that carries testosterone in the blood, to work out how much is free to act.

Now the two letters come back. Once testosterone is confirmed low, **LH and FSH tell you where the fault is**.

- **High LH and FSH**: the brain is shouting and the testes aren't answering. That's *primary* hypogonadism, a problem in the testes themselves (an old injury, mumps, chemotherapy, a genetic condition such as Klinefelter syndrome).
- **Low or normal LH and FSH**: the testes would work if anyone called. That's *secondary* hypogonadism, a quiet brain. Obesity, opioids, high prolactin (the hormone at /apothecary/prolactin), past steroid use, serious illness and pituitary problems all live here.

The second group is the interesting one, because much of it can be undone.

## Reversible causes first

Here's a car analogy. If the dashboard says the battery is low, a good mechanic checks whether you've left the lights on before he sells you a new battery. Testosterone works the same way.

**Sleep.** In a 2011 study in JAMA, Rachel Leproult and Eve Van Cauter at the University of Chicago restricted ten healthy young men to five hours a night for one week. Their daytime testosterone fell by roughly 10 to 15 percent, the kind of drop normally linked to ten or more years of ageing. Untreated sleep apnoea does similar damage night after night.

**Weight.** Belly fat is hormonally busy. It holds an enzyme, aromatase, that converts testosterone into oestrogen, and the extra oestrogen and inflammation tell the brain to send less LH. Low testosterone then makes it easier to store fat, and the circle tightens. Losing weight tends to raise testosterone, often meaningfully.

**Alcohol.** Heavy drinking poisons Leydig cells directly and strains the liver, which handles the hormone's transport protein.

**Opioids**, including prescribed painkillers, sit on receptors in the hypothalamus and mute its pulse signal. Long-term users very often run low.

Add uncontrolled diabetes, some medicines (steroids for asthma or arthritis), and anabolic steroids taken in the past, which can leave the brain's signal suppressed long after the last injection. The NHS makes the same point in its page on the so-called "male menopause": lifestyle and stress explain many of these symptoms in midlife men, with true hormone failure less common.

> Before you replace a hormone, find out who switched it off.

Which brings us to the replacement itself, and its strangest side effect.

## What TRT does to sperm

Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) works for men who truly need it. It usually restores desire, can lift mood and energy, and builds muscle and bone. In 2023 the large TRAVERSE trial, led by Michael Lincoff, followed over 5,000 middle-aged and older men with low testosterone and heart risk. Testosterone did not raise major heart attacks or strokes against placebo, though atrial fibrillation, blood clots in the lungs and kidney injury were more common.

Now the loop from the top. When testosterone arrives from outside, the brain sees plenty in the blood and stops sending LH and FSH. The testes lose their orders. Testosterone *inside* the testes, which needs to be far higher than in the blood to make sperm, collapses. Sperm production drops, often to zero.

So **TRT works as a male contraceptive**, an unreliable one but a powerful one. The Endocrine Society advises against it for men who hope to father a child soon. Recovery after stopping is common but can take many months, and isn't guaranteed. Doctors also watch red blood cell counts, which testosterone pushes up, along with the prostate and breathing at night.

## When to see someone

See a doctor if low desire and fewer morning erections have lasted for months, especially with any of the specific signs: breast swelling, smaller testes, loss of body hair, hot flushes. Go sooner if you've had testicular injury, chemotherapy or long-term opioid or steroid use, or if you and your partner have been trying for a baby without luck.

Ask for a fasting morning testosterone, and expect a repeat if it's low. If it's confirmed, ask for LH, FSH and, often, prolactin, iron studies or a pituitary check, depending on the answer. Mention your sleep, your alcohol, your waist and every medicine, because those are the first places a good doctor will look.

And tell the doctor if you're thinking of starting a family before anyone writes a prescription. That one sentence changes the plan.
