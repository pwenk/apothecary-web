Count the tabs open on your laptop right now. Somewhere among them is the thing you sat down to do, wedged between a flight you will never book and an article about octopus intelligence you opened at 1 a.m.

Now think of a deadline three weeks away. Feel anything? Move it to tomorrow at nine and your whole body wakes up like a dog hearing car keys.

By the end of this page you'll know which chemical runs low in which corner of your skull, the six questions a doctor uses to open the conversation, and why the medicine that calms a racing mind is, of all things, a stimulant. You may also recognise yourself. Or the person who sleeps next to you.

## The kid grew up and sat still

ADHD (attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder) is a pattern of inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity that starts in childhood and gets in the way of life. The psychiatrists' manual, DSM-5, lists nine signs in each of two columns. Children need six from a column. Adults, from 17 on, need five, plus signs that started before age 12, show up in at least two areas of life, and have lasted six months or more.

In grown-up form the inattentive column reads like a confession. The email you will answer "properly, later". The careless slip in the contract you read twice. Keys, wallet, the thread of a conversation while the other person is still talking. Five projects at 80 percent and none at 100.

The hyperactive column changes shape with age. NIMH notes that inattention tends to persist while hyperactivity tends to fade, and in practice much of it simply moves indoors. The boy who climbed the bookshelf becomes the man whose knee bounces under the table, who finishes your sentences for you, who quits a job on a Tuesday because Tuesday felt long.

Two things the manual never lists describe the inner weather better than anything in it.

## Time blindness and the tunnel

The psychologist Russell Barkley has argued for decades that ADHD is a disorder of time and self-control more than of attention. People with it usually know exactly what to do. What they can't do is feel the future tugging on the present. Barkley calls this time blindness. Time comes in two sizes, **now and not now**, and "not now" weighs nothing until it collapses into "now".

That is why the far deadline is silent and the near one is a fire alarm. Panic becomes the engine. Plenty of adults with ADHD have built whole careers on last-minute adrenaline without knowing that's what they were running on.

Then comes the part that confuses everyone. Hand the same person a problem they find fascinating and they vanish into it for nine hours and forget to eat. That's hyperfocus. In a 2019 study of more than 600 adults, Kathleen Hupfeld and colleagues at the Universities of Florida and Michigan found that people with more ADHD symptoms reported more of it, at school, in hobbies and in front of screens.

So there is plenty of attention in the tank. The hard part is steering it, and that brings us to feelings.

## The feelings nobody lists

The DSM criteria are almost all about behaviour. Ask adults with ADHD what hurts most and many will talk about emotions instead.

**Emotional dysregulation** is the clinical name for it: anger that arrives at full volume, frustration that tips over in seconds, a mood that swings on one small event. A 2014 review in the American Journal of Psychiatry by Philip Shaw and colleagues found it common across the whole lifespan in ADHD and a major source of the trouble it causes. It still isn't a formal criterion.

Then there's the one you've met on TikTok: rejection sensitive dysphoria. The Denver psychiatrist William Dodson popularised the phrase for the sudden, outsized pain some people with ADHD feel at criticism or the faintest hint of rejection. One raised eyebrow from a boss can poison a week. Treat it as a popular label: it is not in the DSM or the ICD, and formal research on it is thin. The experience it names is real enough to the people who have it.

A small slight, a huge reaction. Which raises the next question: how does someone carry all this for 30 years without a name for it?

## Where it hides

It hides in girls. NIMH says plainly that ADHD in girls and women is especially likely to have been missed in childhood. Girls more often get the inattentive version: the quiet daydreamer by the window who disrupts nothing and loses everything. Many are treated for anxiety or depression first, sometimes for decades, while the engine underneath goes unnamed.

It hides in clever people. A sharp mind can compensate for years. You cram the night before and still get the top mark, you charm your way past the forgotten deadline. The scaffolding holds until life adds a load it can't carry, often a first real job, a baby, or self-employment with no boss to supply the panic.

The numbers show how many arrive late. A CDC survey in late 2023 estimated that 15.5 million American adults, 6 percent, had a current ADHD diagnosis, and more than half of them got it at 18 or older.

So what exactly is the brain short of?

## The brake behind your forehead

Behind your forehead sits the prefrontal cortex, the brain's air-traffic control. It holds a goal in mind, filters out noise, and brakes whatever impulse is shouting loudest. It runs on two chemical messengers, dopamine and noradrenaline, and it is fussy about the dose. The Yale neuroscientist Amy Arnsten has shown that it works best at a middle level: too little (boredom, fatigue) and the signal drowns, too much (stress) and it seizes up. Interest is what brings it to the sweet spot.

That one finding explains the tunnel and the deadline. A fascinating problem or a looming disaster pushes a low-running system up into its working range. A dull tax form never does.

ADHD brains seem to sit at the low end. In a 2009 PET-scan study in JAMA, Nora Volkow's team found less dopamine machinery (the pumps and receptors that handle it) along the reward pathway of 53 unmedicated adults with ADHD than in 44 people without it. The lower the markers, the worse the attention.

Picture a car with good brakes and low brake fluid. The pedal is there, you press it, and not much happens. Hence the interrupted sentences, the 2 a.m. purchase, and the boring task your brain refuses to fund because the dopamine payout is too small.

Genes do most of the loading. Twin and family studies put ADHD's heritability at about 74 percent, which is why a child's diagnosis so often makes a parent go quiet in the doctor's office.

Now the question from the top: why hand a speeding brain a stimulant?

## Why speed calms you

Because the part that needs more signal is the brake.

After a nerve cell releases dopamine or noradrenaline, little pumps pull it back in. Stimulants like methylphenidate (Ritalin) block those pumps, so the messenger lingers in the gap and the message lands harder. Amphetamines block the pumps and also push extra dopamine out. Volkow's group showed in 2001, in 11 healthy volunteers, that ordinary oral doses of methylphenidate measurably raise dopamine in the human brain. In a prefrontal cortex that runs low, that top-up lets the brake bite again, and the restlessness quiets because control comes back online.

The costs are real: less appetite, worse sleep, a faster heart, and a potential for misuse, which is why these drugs are tightly controlled and why a doctor checks your heart and history before prescribing. Non-stimulant options and therapy exist too, and NIMH lists both as standard for adults.

> ADHD gives you plenty of attention and very little say over where it lands.

## When to see someone

Start with a self-check. The WHO Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale, ASRS v1.1, opens with a six-question screener built by Ronald Kessler's team at Harvard in 2005. You rate how often each thing has happened over the past six months, and four or more answers in the shaded boxes means it's worth a proper look. In its first validation it caught roughly seven in ten real cases and almost never rang for someone without ADHD. A high score is a reason to book the appointment. It diagnoses nothing.

The real assessment takes time, usually with a psychiatrist or clinical psychologist. Expect a long interview about your life now and your childhood, rating scales, and a request for old school reports or a parent or partner who can describe you from the outside. A good clinician also rules out the look-alikes that fog attention: poor sleep or sleep apnoea, an underactive thyroid, depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, and heavy alcohol or cannabis use.

Go if the pattern goes back to childhood, shows up both at work and at home, and costs you money, jobs or love. Go sooner if you've been quietly medicating yourself with coffee, nicotine or deadline panic.

Some signals are a temperament you've carried all your life. Others are the body raising its hand for the first time, quietly, and keeping it raised for three weeks.
