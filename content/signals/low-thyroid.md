Touch your fingertips to your neck. If they feel like someone else's cold hands, keep reading.

There's a gland under that spot, shaped like a bow tie, that decides how fast every cell in your body burns. When it slows, you feel it everywhere at once and nowhere in particular, which is why so many people spend years being told they're just tired.

By the end of this page you'll know the five things the blood test measures, the eyebrow sign doctors half-believe, and why two famous outsiders told people to throw out the lab report and reach for a thermometer.

## The gland that sets the pace

The thyroid makes two hormones from iodine. T4 is the bulk product, a storage form. T3 is the active one, made mostly by snipping one iodine atom off T4 inside your tissues.

T3 walks into a cell and turns up the furnace. It tells the cell to build more mitochondria (the little power plants), run more sodium pumps, and burn more fuel. A side effect of all that work is heat. **Your body temperature is, in part, a thyroid readout.**

So when thyroid output falls, everything that depends on burning slows down. That turns out to be nearly everything, and it explains a list of symptoms that looks random until you see the furnace behind it.

## What slow feels like

**Cold.** Cold hands and feet, the jumper in July, the partner who keeps turning the heating up. Less burning means less heat.

**Tiredness** that sleep doesn't touch, and thinking that feels wrapped in cotton wool. Low mood often comes along.

**Dry skin and coarse hair.** Skin cells turn over more slowly and oil glands go quiet. Hair thins, sometimes falls out in clumps.

**Constipation.** The gut's muscle runs on the same furnace and simply moves more lazily.

**A slow pulse.** T3 makes the heart more sensitive to adrenaline. Take it away and the heart idles low, sometimes under 60 beats a minute at rest.

Add a few kilos of weight gain (much of it water and puffiness), a puffy face in the morning, heavier periods, and cholesterol that creeps up because the liver pulls less of it out of the blood. NIDDK estimates that about 5 in 100 Americans over 12 have an underactive thyroid, most cases mild, and women are far more likely to have it than men.

Then there's the eyebrow.

## The eyebrow sign

Look at the outer third of your eyebrows, the tail that runs toward your temple. If it has thinned or vanished, old textbooks call it the **Hertoghe sign**, after the Belgian doctor Eugène Hertoghe, who wrote about thyroid patients a little over a century ago.

Treat it as folk-ish. It does appear in people with low thyroid, and it also appears with age, plucking, eczema and a dozen other things. A doctor may notice it, smile, and order the blood test anyway.

The blood test is where most of the frustration lives, so let's open it.

## What the blood test measures

Think of a home thermostat. The pituitary gland at the base of the brain watches thyroid levels and, when they fall, shouts louder for more. That shout is **TSH** (thyroid-stimulating hormone).

- **TSH** goes *up* when the thyroid is failing, because the pituitary is shouting. It's the first test almost everywhere and the most sensitive. A high TSH with normal T4 is called subclinical hypothyroidism, usually mild.
- **Free T4** is the unbound, usable T4 in the blood. Low free T4 with high TSH is the classic picture of an underactive gland.
- **Free T3** is the active hormone. The American Thyroid Association notes it is more useful for an overactive thyroid. In low thyroid it's often the last number to fall.
- **TPO antibodies** (and sometimes thyroglobulin antibodies) show the immune system attacking the gland. That is **Hashimoto's disease**, the commonest cause of low thyroid in countries with enough iodine. Antibodies can sit there for years before TSH budges, so a positive result is a reason to retest over time.

One practical note from the ATA: biotin, the popular hair-and-nail supplement, can scramble several thyroid tests. Tell whoever draws your blood if you use it.

Treatment, when the numbers say so, is usually levothyroxine, a synthetic T4 that the body converts to T3 as needed. The ATA's 2014 guideline names it the standard, with not enough evidence to recommend routine T4-plus-T3 combinations.

That should be the end of the story. For many people it's where the anger starts.

## Why "normal" makes people furious

Three reasons, all real.

First, the symptoms are vague. Fatigue, cold, low mood and constipation also come from poor sleep, low iron, depression, a heavy week and winter. A normal TSH genuinely rules out the thyroid for most of them, which is good news delivered in an annoying tone.

Second, the reference range is a population range. A TSH around 0.4 to 4 counts as normal in many labs, yet each person seems to have a narrower set point of their own inside it. Normal for the crowd and normal for you can differ.

Third, some treated patients still feel unwell. In a 2002 UK community study, Ponnusamy Saravanan and colleagues surveyed hundreds of people on levothyroxine with a normal TSH and found worse well-being scores than in matched controls: about a third scored as psychologically distressed, against a quarter of controls. Nobody knows exactly why, and combination T3 trials have mostly disappointed.

> A normal blood test can be true and still leave you cold.

Into that gap stepped two men with thermometers.

## Barnes, Peat and the thermometer

**Broda Barnes**, an American physician, argued from the 1940s onward that low thyroid was vastly underdiagnosed. In his 1976 book *Hypothyroidism: The Unsuspected Illness* he told readers to tuck a thermometer in the armpit on waking, before getting out of bed, and to read a low morning temperature as a sign of a slow thyroid, whatever the blood said.

**Ray Peat**, the biologist with the cult following, went further. In his view a person's warmth and resting pulse, along with how they feel, tell you more about thyroid function than TSH does. He wrote that lab ranges were drawn too wide, that stress hormones and polyunsaturated fats suppress thyroid activity, and that many people called healthy are running slow. His essay *Thyroid: Therapies, Confusion, and Fraud* lays it out; the full school gets its own page at /schools/ray-peat.

What holds up: temperature and pulse do fall in real hypothyroidism, because the furnace is the mechanism. What doesn't: basal temperature has never been shown in good studies to diagnose thyroid disease, and it shifts with sleep, infection, room heat, the menstrual cycle and where you put the thermometer. Treating a low reading with thyroid hormone when the blood is normal risks the opposite problem, an overdriven heart and thinning bones.

## When to see someone

Ask for a TSH test if several of these have crept in together over months: cold you can't shake, tiredness that sleep doesn't touch, dry skin, constipation, thinning hair, a slow pulse, heavy periods, or low mood. Ask sooner if thyroid disease runs in your family, you have another autoimmune condition (type 1 diabetes, coeliac disease, vitiligo), you're pregnant or trying, or you've had neck radiation or thyroid surgery.

If the result is borderline, ask for free T4 and TPO antibodies, and for a repeat in a few months. If it's normal and you still feel slow, the useful next questions are about iron, B12, vitamin D, sleep apnoea and mood. Keep a simple log of morning temperature and resting pulse if you like data. Show it to the doctor as a clue, and treat it as nothing more.

The thyroid sets the speed of every cell. In men, a second hormone sets something closer to the appetite for life, and it hides its own decline behind the same tiredness. Next: low testosterone, the morning sign men notice first, and the treatment that quietly switches off their sperm.
