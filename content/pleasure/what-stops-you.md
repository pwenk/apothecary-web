Most people who want better sex go shopping for a technique. A new position, a supplement, a book with a ridiculous title.

Usually something is already standing in the doorway, and no technique gets past it. There are six usual suspects. One lives in an old Sunday school memory, one in your browser history, and one is so polite you've probably never suspected it.

By the end of this page you'll know which one is yours, and the single place where all six lose their grip at once.

## Suspect one: shame and the old religion

Somewhere early, many people learned that desire is dirty. Maybe a church said it, maybe a parent's face did, maybe nobody said anything and the silence did the teaching. The lesson settles in the body as a flinch that arrives just as pleasure starts.

Yohami calls the "lust is lack" idea an inversion, a way of hating your own sexuality while sounding wise. He holds the opposite view: lust is beautiful, and sex sits so high that it's easy to drag low.

Research backs the weight of belief. In 2019 Joshua Grubbs and colleagues reviewed the studies on people who feel addicted to porn and found that **the feeling of addiction is strongly tied to moral disapproval**, often religious, and not only to how much people actually watch. The conflict between what you do and what you were taught can do more damage than the behaviour itself.

**Practice:** write down one desire you've never said aloud, in plain words, and read it to yourself slowly. Notice where your body tightens. Then ask whose voice the tightening belongs to, and whether you'd still choose that voice today.

## Suspect two: approval-seeking

This one wants to be liked so badly it hides what it wants. Yohami describes a reader who realised he'd been hiding his desire from women to seem safe and likeable, and so every conversation came across as neutered. When desire finally showed up, she was confused and left.

Yohami's reply was to put your desire on the table as if nothing in the world depended on the outcome. **Approval-seeking makes you a pleasant stranger**, and pleasant strangers rarely get invited in.

**Practice:** ask for small things you want every day until someone says no. A better table, a discount, a stranger's recommendation, a date. Each no teaches the body that refusal costs nothing, and desire gets braver.

## Suspect three: porn and the dopamine loop

Dopamine is the molecule of wanting, and it loves novelty. Porn offers endless novelty with zero effort and zero risk of rejection. That combination trains the reward system toward a screen and away from a person, who is slower, realer and occasionally says no.

In 2014 Simone Kühn and Jürgen Gallinat in Berlin scanned 64 men and found that more hours of porn a week went with **less grey matter in the right caudate**, part of the brain's reward circuitry, and a weaker response to sexual images in a neighbouring reward area. The study can't tell cause from effect. Maybe porn shapes the brain, or maybe men with that brain seek more stimulation. Either way, the loop is real.

Yohami's description fits: an addiction is you on autopilot managing old pain, and an autopilot is a choice you forgot you're making.

**Practice:** when the urge arrives, sit with it for ninety seconds before doing anything. Find where it lives in the body. Usually there's a feeling underneath, boredom or loneliness or stress, and once you've felt it, the choice becomes a choice again.

## Suspect four: performance anxiety

The man who watches himself perform has stopped feeling. Masters and Johnson called it spectatoring: one part of you is having sex, the other is up on the ceiling taking notes.

It's very common. Robert Pyke's 2020 review estimates that **sexual performance anxiety affects 9 to 25% of men** and 6 to 16% of women, and calls it a cause or sustainer of most common sexual problems. Fear of losing an erection makes losing it likelier; fear of finishing early makes it faster.

**Practice:** sensate focus, the classic from Masters and Johnson. For a week, take turns touching each other with intercourse and orgasm off the table. The only task is noticing sensation. With nothing to achieve, the ceiling empties and the body comes back.

## Suspect five: the nice-guy contract

This is the polite one. In *No More Mr. Nice Guy* (2000), the therapist Robert Glover described what he called a covert contract. It runs roughly like this: **I'll be good to you, so you'll give me what I want.** It's signed silently and never shown to the other person. He listens, helps, compliments, waits, and then resents her for not paying up.

Yohami puts it more bluntly: treating someone "too well" is dishonest, because the excess is a bid for something, usually validation. Women feel the hidden invoice even when they can't name it, and it rarely makes them want him.

**Practice:** catch yourself in one generous act this week and finish the sentence "I'm doing this so that she'll…". Then either ask for that thing directly, or do the act for its own sake and let the invoice go.

> The kindest thing you can offer someone in bed is a desire with nothing hidden behind it.

## Suspect six: living in your head

The last suspect is the quietest. Some people spend their entire sex lives slightly above their own bodies, thinking about it while it happens.

A man on X described opening up fully during sex for the first time at 51. Only then did he understand why earlier girlfriends had called the sex mechanical: he'd had no frame of reference for anything else. Plenty of people go decades without noticing the gap.

Yohami's teasing line is the right medicine here: out-of-body experiences are fun, and in-your-body experiences are mind-blowing.

**Practice:** before you touch someone, take three slow breaths into the belly and name three sensations silently: the air on your arms, the weight of your feet, the warmth of their skin. For something sillier, Yohami suggests ordering a Coke Zero and pretending it's spiked. The body loosens when it believes it's allowed.

## The way back is the body

Now the loop from the top. Your suspect is whichever one made you wince while you read. Many people have two or three.

And the single place all six lose their grip is the body. Each suspect shows up first as a sensation: a flinch, a tight chest before you ask, an itch under a feeling, a held breath, or no sensation at all. **Every practice above works the same way**: it sends your attention back into the body, where the suspect can be felt, and once it's felt it starts to loosen.

Everything in this wing, polarity and frame, surrender, rope, the slow Taoist rhythm, works only for someone actually inside their body when it happens.

## Before you try anything

If erections, desire or orgasm have changed suddenly, see a doctor first, because circulation, hormones and medicines can all play a part. If shame or compulsive use is running your life, a sex therapist who works with these issues can help faster than a page can.
