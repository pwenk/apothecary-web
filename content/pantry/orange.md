Your pill box may warn you about grapefruit. Almost everyone blames a bitter molecule called naringin, and almost everyone has the wrong suspect.

The sweet orange carries naringin's cousin, a molecule that really does tamper with how some drugs get absorbed, just more quietly. By the end you'll know which citrus to keep away from which pills, and why the white pith you peel off is the richest part of the fruit.

## Macro: the fruit and the juice are two different foods

One medium orange gives you 62 kcal, 15 g of carbohydrate, 12 g of sugar and 3 g of fiber. That fiber sits in the membranes and pulp, a mesh that holds the sugar like a sponge holds water, so it seeps into your blood slowly.

A cup of fresh juice is roughly two to three oranges with the mesh thrown away: 112 kcal, 21 g of sugar and half a gram of fiber, according to USDA data. You drink it in thirty seconds.

The body notices. In 2013, Isao Muraki and the Harvard cohort team followed about 187,000 nurses and health professionals for years. More whole fruit went with slightly lower type 2 diabetes risk. More fruit juice went the other way: each extra three servings a week came with about **8% higher risk**. Observational data can't prove cause, but the direction is consistent.

## Micro: the vitamin C you came for

One orange covers 77% of your Daily Value for vitamin C, about 70 mg. Vitamin C is the cofactor your body needs to build collagen, which is why sailors without fresh fruit got scurvy: gums bled and old wounds reopened. In 1747 the naval surgeon James Lind gave citrus to sick sailors on HMS *Salisbury*, and they recovered fast.

The rest of the micro view is modest and useful. A tenth of your folate and thiamin, small amounts of potassium, copper and calcium. Juice actually wins on some of these by the cup, because a cup holds more fruit; the cost is the sugar arriving all at once.

> The fiber is the brake, and juice is an orange with the brake cut.

## The rest: hesperidin, naringin, and the grapefruit story told straight

Oranges are full of flavanones, bitter-to-neutral plant molecules. The USDA flavonoid database lists about 27 mg of hesperetin and 15 mg of naringenin per 100 g of orange, measured as the core molecule. In a sweet orange these sit mostly as **hesperidin** and **narirutin**. Grapefruit carries **naringin** instead, which is the one that makes it bitter. Most of this chemistry lives in the pith and membranes, which is why juice ends up with less: the USDA puts juice at about 12 mg of hesperetin per 100 g.

Now the drug story, in two parts.

**Part one, the big one: furanocoumarins.** The famous grapefruit effect comes from furanocoumarins such as bergamottin. They permanently switch off CYP3A4, an enzyme in the gut wall that normally destroys part of a drug before it reaches your blood. With the enzyme gone, more drug gets through. David Bailey's group lists more than 85 drugs that interact, 43 of them seriously, and one glass can still matter a day later. Naringin is not the culprit here. Sweet oranges such as navel and Valencia contain no furanocoumarins and don't cause this effect; in one 2001 study, plain orange juice was the control. Seville (bitter) oranges, limes and pomelos do cause it.

**Part two, the quiet one: OATP transporters.** Some drugs need a door to get *into* the gut wall, a transporter called OATP1A2. Here naringin and hesperidin do act. Bailey's team showed in 2007 that naringin blocks this door, and that hesperidin from orange blocks it about as strongly in the lab. Earlier, in 2002, large amounts of grapefruit, orange or apple juice cut absorption of fexofenadine, an allergy drug, to 30 to 40% of normal.

So grapefruit can push some drugs *up* (enzyme off) and others *down* (door shut). Sweet orange only shares the door effect.

## In the Apothecary's opinion

Eat the orange, pith and all; it's a cheap, excellent source of vitamin C with its own sugar brake. Treat juice as a sweet drink with vitamins in it, lovely in a small glass with food.

Before you mix citrus and medicine: if a drug label mentions grapefruit, treat Seville orange, lime and pomelo the same way, and ask your pharmacist whether your pills ride the OATP door too. Orange juice and some drugs, like fexofenadine, are worth spacing apart.
