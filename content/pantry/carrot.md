During the Second World War, the story goes, Britain told the world its night-fighter pilots saw in the dark because they ate carrots. Whatever the propaganda was hiding, it stuck: generations of children were told carrots would let them see at night.

The carrot does hold one vision molecule in staggering amounts, and a second molecule that kills fungi and, in rats, slowed colon cancer. It also became the center of a raw salad ritual. By the end you'll know which of the three claims a carrot can actually back.

## Macro: sweet, light, crunchy water

A medium raw carrot is 25 kcal: about 6 g of carbohydrate, half of it sugar, and 1.7 g of fiber. It's almost 90% water. Protein and fat are rounding errors.

The sugar is mostly sucrose, which is why carrots taste sweeter than most roots. The fiber is the interesting part for this story. Raw carrot fiber is tough, and a good share of it reaches the colon barely changed.

## Micro: one root, half your vitamin A

One carrot gives you about 509 µg of vitamin A activity, 57% of your Daily Value. Nothing else in the micro view comes close: a bit of vitamin K, B6, potassium and vitamin C, each in single digits.

Vitamin A is the night-vision link that the propaganda borrowed. Your retina uses a vitamin A molecule called retinal to catch light; without enough of it, you go night-blind. So carrots do protect vision **if you're deficient**. If you already have enough, extra carrots don't sharpen anything.

> The carrot's vision magic only works on eyes that were short of vitamin A to begin with.

## The rest: carotene, falcarinol, and the salad

**Beta-carotene** is the orange pigment, about 5 mg per carrot, plus 2 mg of alpha-carotene. Plants make it; your gut wall splits it into vitamin A, and only as fast as you need it. That's why you can't poison yourself with vitamin A from carrots the way you can with liver or pills. Eat a great many and your skin may turn faintly orange, called carotenemia, which is harmless and fades.

**Falcarinol** is the strange one. It belongs to a family called polyacetylenes that the carrot family uses as a natural pesticide against fungi. The Danish researcher Morten Kobaek-Larsen and colleagues fed falcarinol and its relative falcarindiol to rats given a colon-cancer-causing chemical. In a 2019 study, precancerous spots and tumors dropped as the dose rose, and inflammation markers such as COX-2 fell. That's rats, not people. Falcarinol is also sensitive to heat: in one Danish study, boiling carrot for 12 minutes cut it by about 70%.

USDA doesn't list falcarinol, so it isn't in the block above.

Then the salad. Ray Peat, the biologist behind the "pro-metabolic" school (see [Ray Peat](/schools/ray-peat)), wrote that fibrous foods carrying antibacterial chemicals, "such as bamboo shoots or raw carrots", help reduce endotoxin and serotonin from the gut. Endotoxin is a piece of bacterial wall that can leak into the blood and stir inflammation. His followers turned this into a daily ritual: a raw carrot grated into long strips, dressed with coconut oil, vinegar and salt. The idea is that the tough fiber sweeps through the gut, carrying bacteria and their byproducts out, while the oil and vinegar add their own antibacterial touch.

What holds up: carrot fiber largely survives digestion, and the falcarinol family is antimicrobial. What doesn't yet: no controlled human trial has tested this salad for endotoxin, estrogen, serotonin or anything else.

## In the Apothecary's opinion

A cheap, excellent source of vitamin A, and a raw vegetable you'll actually eat. The carrot salad is harmless and possibly good for digestion, with its grand claims still untested; whether it works for you, your gut will tell you within a couple of weeks.

If you have a sensitive gut or IBS, raw fiber can cramp; start small and ask a doctor if it hurts.
