Around 400 BC, a Greek army of ten thousand was marching home along the Black Sea coast near what is now Trabzon in Turkey. The soldiers found beehives, ate the honey, and collapsed. The historian Xenophon, who was there, described men vomiting, raving and lying on the ground as if after a defeat.

Within a few days they were marching again. By the end of this page you'll know what was in that honey, and what's in yours.

## Macro: sugar, dressed beautifully

A tablespoon of honey is 64 kcal and 17 g of sugar. Nothing else in the macro view registers: no fat, no fiber, a trace of protein.

Bees make it by collecting nectar, mostly sucrose, and adding an enzyme that splits it into its two halves, fructose and glucose. Then they fan it with their wings until most of the water evaporates. What's left is about 82% sugar.

Compared with table sugar, honey has slightly fewer calories per spoon only because it carries some water. Your body handles the fructose and glucose almost the same way whether they came from a hive or a refinery.

## Micro: the emptiest page in the pantry

Look at the micro block. Every vitamin and mineral rounds to 0 or 1% of the Daily Value. Honey's mineral and vitamin claims are true only in the tiny sense that they're present.

That's worth sitting with. Honey's reputation as a "natural superfood" comes from somewhere, but it doesn't come from this view. To find what honey actually does, you need the third one.

## The rest: the cough, the poison, and the spores

**Coughs.** Honey's best-tested trick is soothing children's coughs. The 2018 Cochrane review, which pooled six trials with 899 children aged one to eighteen, found honey **probably reduces cough frequency** better than no treatment or placebo, with moderate-certainty evidence. It held up about as well as dextromethorphan, a common cough-syrup ingredient. The thick sweet coating on an irritated throat probably explains part of it.

**Why it keeps.** Honey has so little water that microbes trying to grow in it get dried out by osmosis, the pull of water toward sugar. It's also acidic, and the bee enzyme glucose oxidase produces small amounts of hydrogen peroxide when honey is diluted, which is part of why honey has been put on wounds for millennia.

**The poison.** Back to the Black Sea. Bees there collect nectar from certain rhododendron species, and that nectar carries **grayanotoxins**, molecules that jam sodium channels in nerves and heart muscle so the cells can't reset. Poisoning typically drops blood pressure and slows the heart; an expert quoted by Texas A&M adds vomiting, loss of consciousness and seizures. Only a few of the more than 700 rhododendron species seem to produce it, and locals still sell this "mad honey" as a folk remedy and a high. Xenophon's soldiers simply had a bad batch from the wrong hillside.

**The spores.** Honey can carry spores of *Clostridium botulinum*, the bacterium that makes botulinum toxin. Older children and adults have a mature gut that keeps spores from growing. A baby's gut doesn't, so the spores can sprout and make toxin inside them. That's why the CDC and pediatric groups say no honey before 12 months, including on pacifiers.

## In the Apothecary's opinion

A delicious sugar with a few genuine tricks: a spoon for a child's cough is backed by decent evidence, and honey's preservation chemistry is real. As nutrition it's sugar, and should be counted like sugar.

Never give honey to babies under one year. Be wary of "mad honey" sold online. If you have diabetes, honey raises blood sugar like any other sugar.
