There is dopamine in the banana on your counter. Real dopamine, the molecule your brain releases when you see a message from someone you want. A Japanese lab measured it in 2000 and found milligrams of it in the flesh.

You'll eat it, and you'll feel nothing. By the end of this page you'll know why, and which part of the banana actually does something to you.

## Macro: a sugar that used to be starch

One medium banana carries 105 kcal, almost all of it carbohydrate: 27 g, of which 14 g is sugar and 3 g is fiber. Protein and fat barely register.

The sugar is a recent arrival. A banana is picked green and hard, packed with starch, and it ripens on the way to you. Enzymes take the starch apart and turn it into glucose, fructose and sucrose. That's why a brown-spotted banana tastes like candy and a green one tastes like chalk.

In 1986, Hans Englyst and John Cummings fed bananas of different ripeness to people with an ileostomy, an opening that lets doctors collect what leaves the small intestine. The least ripe fruit was 37% starch by dry weight, the ripest only 3%. **Up to 90% of the green banana's starch came out undigested**, which means in a normal gut it would travel on to the colon.

So the same fruit is fast sugar on Friday and slow fiber-like starch on Monday, depending only on when you peel it. Hold that thought; it comes back in the third view.

## Micro: the potassium legend, corrected

Everyone knows bananas mean potassium. One gives you 422 mg, which is 9% of the Daily Value. Respectable, and nowhere near a jackpot. A cup of orange juice gives you a little more.

The banana's real micro talent is **vitamin B6**: 0.43 mg, a quarter of the Daily Value in one fruit. B6 is the helper your body uses to turn amino acids into neurotransmitters, including the dopamine and serotonin we're about to meet. Add 14% of your manganese, 11% of your vitamin C and 10% of your copper, and the banana starts to look like a quiet overachiever.

Potassium matters for a physical reason. Every nerve and muscle cell keeps potassium inside and sodium outside, like a battery holding a charge. Your heart beats on that difference. A banana is a small, cheap top-up of the inside half.

> A banana carries the ingredients of a good mood and none of the delivery.

## The rest: brain chemicals with no ticket in

Kazuki Kanazawa's lab at Kobe University found dopamine at 2.5 to 10 mg per 100 g of banana flesh, and a startling 80 to 560 mg in the peel. In the fruit it works as an antioxidant, and it's part of why a bruised banana turns brown so fast.

Then there's serotonin. Feldman and Lee measured it in 1985 at about 15 micrograms per gram, so roughly 1.8 mg in one fruit. Plantains carry twice as much. Doctors know this well: bananas can raise urine levels of 5-HIAA, a serotonin breakdown product that is used to screen for certain tumors, which is why patients are told to skip them before the test.

Here is the loop closing. Neither dopamine nor serotonin can cross the blood-brain barrier, the tight wall of cells around your brain's blood vessels. Your brain makes its own supply from scratch. The banana's dopamine stays in your gut and bloodstream, where it gets broken down. The tryptophan, the raw material your brain *can* use for serotonin, is about 11 mg per banana, far too little to change your mood.

What does reach somewhere is the starch. A greener banana sends more of it to the colon, where bacteria ferment it into short-chain fats that feed the cells of your gut wall. That's the one part of the banana that works on you beyond calories, and it shrinks with every brown spot.

## In the Apothecary's opinion

A good everyday fruit: easy fuel, a solid hit of B6, a decent splash of potassium. The dopamine and serotonin are wonderful trivia and do nothing for your head. If you want the gut-feeding version, eat them on the green side; if you want quick sugar before training, eat them spotted.

One caution: people with kidney disease or on potassium-sparing drugs should ask their doctor how much potassium is fine, bananas included.
