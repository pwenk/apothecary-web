Gelatin is the wobble in jelly, the shine on a good stock, the reason bone broth sets solid in the fridge overnight. It's made from skin, tendon and bone, the parts of the animal most kitchens now throw away.

Some biochemists think throwing them away left a small, steady hole in our diet, one amino acid wide. By the end you'll know its name, how big the hole might be, and why gelatin alone would starve you anyway.

## Macro: protein and nothing else

An envelope of plain gelatin, about 7 g of powder, gives you 23 kcal and 6 g of protein. No fat, no carbohydrate, no fiber. Dry gelatin is around 86% protein and most of the rest is water.

Gelatin is cooked collagen. Collagen is the most abundant protein in your body, the rope that holds skin, tendons, cartilage and bone together. Simmer it for hours and the ropes unwind into strands that tangle back into a soft gel as they cool.

As protein, though, it's lopsided. It carries almost no tryptophan, one of the essential amino acids your body can't make. So gelatin can't stand in for meat, eggs or milk. It's a side dish for your protein.

## Micro: a nearly blank page

Here's a food whose micro view is almost empty. The one exception in USDA data is copper, about 17% of the Daily Value per envelope; selenium adds 5%. Everything else rounds to zero.

That's useful to see once. Not every food earns its place through vitamins, and this one makes no pretense of it. Its whole story sits in the third view.

> Muscle meat feeds the engine; collagen holds the frame together, and we've been eating only the engine.

## The rest: glycine, the amino acid in every third seat

Collagen repeats one pattern over and over: glycine, then two other amino acids, then glycine again. Glycine is the smallest amino acid, and only something that small fits in the tight center of collagen's triple-twisted rope. Proline, often in the second seat, puts kinks in the chain so it can twist. In USDA data, one envelope of gelatin holds 1.3 g of glycine and 0.86 g of proline.

Here is the hole. Enrique Meléndez-Hevia and colleagues added up every source and use of glycine in an adult body in 2009. Their estimate: we make about 3 g a day and eat another 1.5 to 3 g, which may fall **about 10 g a day short** of what collagen building and other uses require. Their conclusion was that glycine is "semi-essential". That's a calculation; nobody has yet measured such a shortfall in people or tied symptoms to it.

Then there's balance. Muscle meat is rich in methionine and low in glycine; gelatin is the reverse. In rodents, diets low in methionine extend lifespan, and glycine seems to soften methionine's effects. In 2019 the US Interventions Testing Program fed mice a diet with 8% glycine and saw a **4 to 6% longer lifespan** in both sexes. A modest effect, so far only in mice.

Glycine also seems to help some people sleep. In a small Japanese study from 2007, volunteers who took glycine before bed reported feeling better rested the next day, possibly because glycine nudges core body temperature down, part of the body's signal to fall asleep. The evidence there is interesting but thin.

## The Peat view

Ray Peat, in his essay "Gelatin, stress, longevity", argued that modern diets carry too much of the amino acids released during stress, especially tryptophan and cysteine, which he called "antimetabolic", meaning they slow the thyroid and metabolism. Gelatin, rich in glycine and lacking tryptophan, was his counterweight. He suggested many inflammatory and degenerative problems improve with gelatin-rich foods.

What holds up: the amino acid profiles are exactly as he describes, and the rodent glycine and methionine data point his way. What doesn't yet: there are no solid human trials showing gelatin lowers inflammation or slows aging, and calling tryptophan "antimetabolic" goes well beyond the evidence.

## In the Apothecary's opinion

A cheap, old-fashioned way to eat the whole animal again, through broth, jelly and slow-cooked cuts with skin and joints. It's sensible as an addition to normal protein, never a replacement.

If you have kidney disease or a doctor has you on a protein limit, count gelatin as protein like any other.
