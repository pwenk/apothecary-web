An egg is a complete construction kit for a chick, sealed in a shell with nothing missing. That alone makes it one of the most interesting things in your fridge.

But the egg's best molecule is one you've probably never read on a label, and most adults fall short of it. There's also a protein in the raw white that steals one of your vitamins. By the end you'll know both names, and which half of the egg is doing the work.

## Macro: small, dense, and almost all protein and fat

A large egg is 72 kcal: 6.3 g of protein, 4.8 g of fat and almost no carbohydrate. The white is nearly pure protein and water. The yolk holds all the fat and most of everything else.

Egg protein has long been the yardstick nutritionists use to grade other proteins, because it carries every essential amino acid in a useful balance. One egg has about half a gram of leucine, the amino acid that acts as the "start building" signal for muscle.

The fat is mostly the unsaturated kind, with roughly a third saturated. Where the hen lived and what she ate changes the yolk's color and fat profile, so two eggs from two farms are not quite the same food.

## Micro: selenium and the B team

The surprise leader is **selenium**, 28% of your Daily Value in one egg. Selenium sits at the heart of enzymes that mop up oxidative damage and help activate thyroid hormone. Then comes a run of B vitamins: B12, riboflavin and pantothenic acid at 15 to 19% each.

The yolk adds vitamin A, a little vitamin D and some iron. None of these numbers are enormous on their own, which is the point. The egg is a broad, quiet supplier, the friend who shows up with a bit of everything.

> Nearly everything that makes an egg worth eating sits in the yolk people throw away.

## The rest: choline, lutein, and the thief in the white

Here is the loop closed. The molecule is **choline**: 147 mg per egg, 27% of the Daily Value. Your body uses it to make acetylcholine, the messenger behind memory and muscle control, and to build the membranes around every cell. It also helps carry fat out of the liver. The NIH notes that most people in the US eat less than the recommended amount. Eggs are among the richest common sources, nearly all of it in the yolk.

Then **lutein and zeaxanthin**, the yellow pigments that give the yolk its color, about 250 µg per egg. In your eye they collect in the macula, the small patch of retina you use for sharp central vision, where they filter blue light. Spinach has far more lutein per bite, yet a 2004 Tufts study found that men absorbed lutein better from eggs than from spinach or supplements. The yolk's fat carries it in.

**Cholesterol** is the old villain: 186 mg per egg, all in the yolk. For decades guidelines told people to cap eggs. In most people the liver adjusts its own production when you eat more, so dietary cholesterol moves blood levels less than once believed, and the 2015–2020 US Dietary Guidelines dropped the old 300 mg daily limit. A minority of people respond more strongly.

Now the thief. Raw egg white carries **avidin**, a protein that grabs biotin (vitamin B7) and won't let go, so your gut can't absorb it. Eating many raw whites over weeks can cause biotin deficiency. Cooking unfolds avidin and ends the theft. That's one more reason the egg is happier in a pan.

## In the Apothecary's opinion

One of the best-value foods there is: complete protein, choline, selenium and lutein in a package that costs very little. Eat the yolk; that's where the good stuff lives. Cook the white if you eat eggs often.

If your doctor has flagged very high LDL or you have diabetes, ask whether your own numbers respond to eggs, since a minority of people's do.
