Aphrodite was born from sea foam, and ever since, people have looked to the sea for desire. Oysters got the job. Casanova supposedly ate dozens for breakfast, and every raw bar on Earth sells them with a wink.

Most aphrodisiac legends collapse on contact with a lab. This one has a mineral standing behind it, and a 1996 experiment that made young men's testosterone fall by three quarters. By the end you'll know what that mineral does, and what it can't do.

## Macro: almost nothing, beautifully

Six medium raw oysters are 43 kcal. You get about 5 g of protein, a little over 1 g of fat and 2 g of carbohydrate, much of it glycogen, the animal version of starch, which adds to the faintly sweet taste.

That's a very light plate. In fuel terms, a dozen oysters weigh in below a banana. Whatever oysters do to you, it isn't about energy.

## Micro: the chart breaks

Here the numbers stop looking like food. Six wild Eastern oysters bring:

- **Zinc**: 33 mg, about 300% of the Daily Value
- **Vitamin B12**: 7.4 µg, about 306%
- **Copper**: 2.4 mg, about 267%

plus 30% of your selenium and 22% of your iron. No other common food comes close on zinc. Zinc sits inside hundreds of enzymes and helps your immune cells, your sense of taste and smell, wound healing and sperm production. B12 keeps nerves and red blood cells working.

Oysters vary. USDA lists Pacific oysters at less than half the zinc per gram of wild Eastern ones, and farmed oysters differ again. Still, any oyster is a zinc mine.

> For a man running low on zinc, a plate of oysters is a repair kit served with a wink.

## The rest: the testosterone experiment, and what it means

Back to the loop. In 1996, Ananda Prasad's team at Wayne State University put four healthy young men on a zinc-restricted diet. After 20 weeks their average testosterone fell from about 40 to about 11 nmol/L, a drop of nearly three quarters. Then they took nine older men who were mildly zinc deficient and gave them zinc for six months. Their testosterone roughly doubled, from 8 to 16 nmol/L.

So zinc really does sit under the legend. Low zinc can mean low testosterone, and fixing a shortfall can restore it. **What zinc can't do is push testosterone above normal** in someone who already has enough. The studies were tiny, and the effect works in one direction: refilling an empty tank.

There's also a twist. Too much zinc over time blocks copper absorption, and low copper can cause anemia and nerve problems. The adult upper limit for zinc is 40 mg a day, and six oysters already land near it. Conveniently, oysters bring their own copper too, so the food balances itself in a way pills don't.

The rest of the third view is quieter: a quarter gram of omega-3 fats, some choline, very little cholesterol.

## The real risk: raw

Raw oysters are filter feeders, straining seawater all day, and they can carry *Vibrio* bacteria. The CDC estimates about 80,000 *Vibrio* infections a year in the US, most from food, and raw oysters are the classic source. One species, *Vibrio vulnificus*, kills about one in five people who get a serious infection. The danger is far higher for people with liver disease, diabetes, cancer, a weakened immune system or who take stomach-acid blockers. Lemon juice and hot sauce don't kill it; cooking does.

## In the Apothecary's opinion

The aphrodisiac legend has a real mineral behind it, and that's rarer than you'd think. For anyone short on zinc, oysters are the most delicious fix there is. For anyone with enough, the effect is mostly romance, candlelight and expectation, which are not nothing.

If you have liver disease, diabetes, a weak immune system, or you're pregnant, eat your oysters cooked. If you take zinc pills, ask a doctor before stacking them on top of oysters.
