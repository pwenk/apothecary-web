Every mammal on Earth starts life drinking milk, and almost every mammal stops. Most humans do too: about two in three adults have lost much of their ability to digest it.

Yet one group of herders' descendants kept going, a dairy company built a fortune on a single amino acid swap in a cow's protein, and a biologist from Oregon argued milk was one of the best things an adult could drink. By the end you'll know which of them has the evidence.

## Macro: a balanced meal in a glass

A cup of whole milk gives you 149 kcal with almost perfect symmetry: 7.7 g of protein, 7.9 g of fat and 11.7 g of carbohydrate. Nature designed it to grow a calf, so it carries a bit of everything.

All of that carbohydrate is one sugar, lactose. A cup holds about 12 g. Skim milk keeps the protein and sugar and drops most of the fat, so the calories fall to roughly half.

The protein comes in two flavors. **Casein**, about 80% of it, curdles in the stomach into soft clumps that release amino acids slowly over hours. **Whey**, the other 20%, stays liquid and arrives fast. Cheese is mostly casein; the yellowish liquid left behind in cheesemaking is the whey.

## Micro: calcium, and the vitamins nobody credits

Calcium is milk's fame: 276 mg per cup, 21% of the Daily Value. Your bones store it, but your nerves and heart need it in the blood every second. When blood calcium dips, your parathyroid glands release parathyroid hormone (PTH), which pulls calcium out of bone to refill the blood. A steady calcium supply keeps that hormone calm.

The quieter stars are the B vitamins. One cup brings 46% of your B12 and a third of your riboflavin, the B2 that helps your cells burn fuel. Add phosphorus, selenium, vitamin A and, in the US and many other countries, added vitamin D. That 16% vitamin D figure is mostly fortification, so check your local carton.

> Milk is a whole nursery packed into one liquid, and adults are drinking from someone else's.

## The rest: lactose, A1, A2, and a casein that may behave like a drug

Lactose needs the enzyme lactase to split into glucose and galactose. Babies make plenty. After weaning, most humans turn the gene down. MedlinePlus puts it at about 65% of the world's population with reduced ability to digest lactose after infancy. Among people of Northern European descent, only about 5% lose it. A mutation near the *LCT* gene keeps the switch on, and it spread in populations with a long history of drinking fresh milk. Undigested lactose reaches the colon, bacteria ferment it, and you get gas and cramps.

Then comes the A1 versus A2 fight. Beta-casein, one of the casein proteins, comes in two common versions that differ by a single amino acid. When the A1 version is digested, it can release a small peptide called **beta-casomorphin-7** (BCM-7), which can act like a weak opioid in the gut. A2 releases much less. Older cattle breeds and many Asian and African herds carry mostly A2; many common Western dairy breeds carry a mix.

The European Food Safety Authority reviewed the claims in 2009 and found no cause-and-effect link between BCM-7 and diseases such as diabetes, heart disease or autism. The gut-comfort claims rest on small studies. If regular milk bloats you and A2 milk doesn't, your belly is a fair judge; the disease claims stay unproven.

## The Peat view

Ray Peat, the biologist behind a whole school of "pro-metabolic" eating (see [Ray Peat](/schools/ray-peat)), put milk and cheese near the center of his diet. His reasoning ran through calcium and PTH: in his essay on calcium he describes how a shortage of calcium or magnesium drives the parathyroid glands to release more hormone, which pulls calcium from bone. In his article on milk, he goes after the popular fears one by one, from "fattening" to allergies, and suggests that stress itself can lower lactase activity.

What holds up: milk is a dense source of calcium, B12, riboflavin and complete protein, and calcium does suppress PTH. What stays speculative: the broader claims about milk protecting metabolism, and the stress-lactase link, which have little direct human evidence.

## In the Apothecary's opinion

A genuinely nutritious food if you digest it. If you don't, fermented forms like yogurt and hard cheese carry far less lactose. A2 milk is a reasonable experiment for a touchy gut and nothing more.

If you live on milk, mention it to your doctor, especially with kidney stones or on drugs such as some antibiotics and thyroid pills, which calcium can bind.
