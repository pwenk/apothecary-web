Your morning cup gives your body about two calories. Nutritionally, it's barely there.

Yet it changes how awake you are within half an hour, and the largest review of coffee studies found heavy drinkers dying *less* often, not more. By the end you'll know what caffeine actually does to your tiredness, and why the filter in your machine matters for your cholesterol.

## Macro: the nothing that wakes you up

A cup of black brewed coffee contains 2 kcal, a third of a gram of protein and no meaningful carbohydrate, sugar or fat. In the macro view, coffee is flavored water.

Everything changes with what you add. A spoon of sugar brings 16 kcal, a splash of whole milk another 20 or so, and a café-sized flavored latte can carry a small meal. The bean itself is innocent.

That's the fuel view done in three lines. The micro view holds a small surprise.

## Micro: a quiet B-vitamin habit

One cup carries 14% of your Daily Value for riboflavin and 12% for pantothenic acid, two B vitamins your cells use to turn food into energy. There's a little niacin too, plus traces of potassium, magnesium and manganese.

That sounds minor until you multiply it. Someone drinking four cups a day gets more than half their riboflavin from coffee without trying. For heavy drinkers, coffee is one of the larger sources of some of these nutrients in the whole diet.

## The rest: caffeine, chlorogenic acids, and the oil in the cup

**Caffeine**: about 95 mg in a standard cup, with wide swings by bean and brew. All day, as your brain works, a molecule called adenosine builds up and docks onto receptors that slow nerve firing. That buildup is a big part of what tiredness *feels* like. Caffeine has nearly the same shape as adenosine, so it sits in those receptors and blocks them. The tiredness is still there, just muted. When the caffeine wears off, the adenosine that piled up meanwhile lands all at once, which is why the crash can feel worse than the start. In a typical adult, half the caffeine is gone in around five hours, longer in pregnancy, so an afternoon cup is still partly in you at midnight.

**Chlorogenic acids** are coffee's main polyphenols, plant molecules that act as antioxidants and may slow sugar absorption. A cup carries somewhere between 70 and 350 mg, depending on the bean and how dark it's roasted, since heat breaks them down. For many people coffee is the biggest polyphenol source they have.

**Cafestol and kahweol** are oily molecules from the bean. Rianne Urgert and Martijn Katan in Wageningen showed they raise LDL cholesterol, and that a paper filter traps them. Scandinavian boiled coffee, French press and Turkish coffee let them through; filter coffee doesn't. Espresso sits in between.

What about the big picture? Robin Poole's 2017 umbrella review in the *BMJ* pulled together more than 200 meta-analyses. Coffee was more often linked to benefit than harm, with the largest drop in risk at three to four cups a day: about 17% lower death from any cause compared with none. These are observational studies, so healthier people may simply drink more coffee. The clear exception was pregnancy, where higher intake went with lower birth weight and pregnancy loss.

## The Peat view

Ray Peat called caffeine "a vitamin-like nutrient" and defended coffee against its reputation. He pointed to studies where caffeine blocked xanthine oxidase, an enzyme that produces free radicals, and to animal work suggesting sugar alongside caffeine prevented some of its harms. He liked coffee with milk and sugar, with food.

What holds up: coffee drinkers do well in large population studies, and caffeine has real biological effects beyond alertness. What's speculative: the "nutrient" framing, and the idea that the effects are protective for everyone regardless of sleep, anxiety or pregnancy.

## In the Apothecary's opinion

A pleasant, probably helpful drink for most adults, at the doses most people already use. Filter it if your LDL runs high. Keep it away from bedtime, because masking tiredness is a loan you repay in sleep.

If you're pregnant, anxious, on certain antidepressants or antibiotics that slow caffeine breakdown, or have heart rhythm issues, talk to a doctor about how much is right.
