The back of a cereal box tracks about fifteen things. The government database behind it tracks around 150. Chemists who went looking in 2019 counted more than 26,000 distinct molecules in what we eat.

So the label shows you a keyhole. This page hands you the key, and by the end you'll know why the most interesting part of a banana is the part nobody prints.

## The first view: macro, or the fuel

Macro means big. These are the nutrients you eat by the gram: protein, carbohydrate (with sugar and fiber inside it) and fat. They are the logs on the fire.

The calorie numbers come from one man. Wilbur Atwater, a chemist at Wesleyan, burned foods in a sealed steel chamber in the 1890s, then fed people and measured what came out the other end. In 1899 he settled on roughly 4 kilocalories per gram of protein, 4 per gram of carbohydrate and 9 per gram of fat. Every label you have ever read still runs on his arithmetic.

The macro view answers one question: how much energy, and in what form? Sugar burns fast, fat burns slow, protein is mostly used to rebuild you. **Fiber is the odd one out**: it counts as carbohydrate, but you barely digest it, so it feeds the bacteria in your gut instead of you.

That's the fire. Now for the sparks that keep it lit.

## The second view: micro, or the tiny helpers

Micro means small: vitamins and minerals, eaten by the milligram or even the microgram (a millionth of a gram). You need almost nothing of them, and without them nothing works. Vitamin B6 helps build neurotransmitters, zinc sits inside hundreds of enzymes, potassium keeps every nerve cell charged.

To make those tiny numbers readable, the US Food and Drug Administration sets one reference amount per nutrient, the **Daily Value**. It is a single round target for adults and children over four. Vitamin C is 90 mg. Potassium is 4,700 mg. Zinc is 11 mg.

The percent you see on a label, the "%DV", is simply the food's amount divided by that target. The FDA's own rule of thumb: 5% or less per serving is low, 20% or more is high. That's all it means. A Daily Value is a ruler, set generously enough to cover most people, and your own needs can sit above or below it.

On this wing, every food carries a small block with these numbers, taken from the USDA's FoodData Central for the plain, raw form. The micro list shows the five to nine nutrients that matter for that food, with the %DV next to each, so you can see at a glance where a food actually pulls its weight.

But the label stops there. The interesting chemistry is only getting started.

> The label tells you what keeps you alive. The rest tells you why one food feels different from another.

## The third view: the rest, or the crowd nobody counts

Here live the molecules that are neither fuel nor official vitamin. The hesperidin that makes orange pith bitter. The caffeine in coffee and its cousin theobromine in chocolate. The glycine that makes gelatin wobble. Dopamine, yes, the actual molecule, sitting in a banana.

Albert-László Barabási and his colleagues called this the dark matter of nutrition. Their 2019 paper in *Nature Food* pointed out that our databases follow roughly 150 nutrients, while more than 26,000 defined biochemicals are known to occur in food, many with documented effects in the body. Nobody measures most of them routinely.

This is where the fun is, for two reasons. First, the rest is where the myths live: chocolate as a love drug, oysters as foreplay, a raw carrot that sweeps hormones out of your gut. Some of these hold up, some collapse the second you check the dose. Second, the rest is where foods start acting like drugs. Grapefruit can change how a statin behaves in your blood. A cup of coffee changes how tired your brain thinks it is.

Each "rest" entry on this wing comes with a short note: what the molecule does, and whether the amount in a normal serving is enough to matter. That second half is the whole game. A molecule can be fascinating and still show up in quantities too small to notice.

## In the Apothecary's opinion

Read a food in three passes. Macro tells you what kind of fuel you're getting. Micro tells you which tiny gaps it fills, and the %DV makes that a one-second glance. The rest tells you the story, and the story is where you should keep a raised eyebrow, because the dose decides everything.

First up is the fruit most of us eat without a second thought, which happens to contain dopamine, serotonin and a starch that changes its whole personality as it ripens. The next page is the [banana](/pantry/banana), and the question is why all that brain chemistry does nothing for your brain.
